1Fractions & percentages

⚖️ Either paper

▶ The Whole Of Fractions In National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

Adding/subtracting fractions: find a common denominator first.

Multiplying: multiply straight across — no common denominator needed.

Dividing: flip the second fraction and multiply.

Percentage increase/decrease: multiply by the decimal multiplier.

  • Increase by 15% → multiply by 1.15
  • Decrease by 15% → multiply by 0.85

Compound (repeated) change: multiply the multiplier repeatedly — $1.15^3$ for three years. Never just add the percentages.

📝 Worked example

A jacket costs £80. It is reduced by 15%, then the sale price is reduced by a further 10%.

First reduction: $80 \times 0.85 = \pounds 68$

Second reduction: $68 \times 0.90 = \pounds 61.20$

Sale price = £61.20

Note: the total reduction is 23.5%, not 25%.

🚨 Exam trap

Adding percentages for repeated change (15% then 10% is not 25%). Also, forgetting to simplify the final fraction, and using the wrong denominator when adding.

Practice

1

Evaluate $\dfrac{2}{3} + \dfrac{1}{4}$

Show answer

Common denominator 12: $\dfrac{8}{12} + \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{11}{12}$

2

A car worth £12,000 depreciates by 12% each year. Find its value after 2 years.

Show answer

Multiplier $= 0.88$. $12000 \times 0.88^2 = 12000 \times 0.7744 = \pounds 9,292.80$

2Surds & indices

✋ Non-calculator

▶ All Of Surds In National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

Simplifying surds: split into a square factor. $\sqrt{72}=\sqrt{36\times2}=6\sqrt{2}$.

Adding surds: only like surds combine. $3\sqrt{2}+5\sqrt{2}=8\sqrt{2}$, but $\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}$ stays as it is.

Rationalising the denominator: multiply top and bottom by the surd. $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{3}} \times \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{6\sqrt{3}}{3}=2\sqrt{3}$.

Index laws:

  • $a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$
  • $a^m \div a^n = a^{m-n}$
  • $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$
  • $a^0 = 1$
  • $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$
  • $a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}$

📝 Worked example

Simplify $\dfrac{\sqrt{50}}{\sqrt{2}}$.

$\sqrt{50}=\sqrt{25\times2}=5\sqrt{2}$

$\dfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = 5$

Answer = 5

🚨 Exam trap

Writing $\sqrt{a+b}=\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$ — this is false. Also forgetting that $a^{-n}$ is a reciprocal, and adding indices when multiplying bases that are different.

Practice

1

Simplify $\sqrt{48}+\sqrt{27}$

Show answer

$\sqrt{48}=4\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{27}=3\sqrt{3}$, so total $=7\sqrt{3}$

2

Evaluate $27^{-\frac{2}{3}}$

Show answer

$27^{1/3}=3$, so $27^{-2/3}=\dfrac{1}{3^2}=\dfrac{1}{9}$

3Scientific notation

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Whole Of Scientific Notation National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

A number is in scientific notation when written as $a\times10^n$ where $1\le a<10$ and $n$ is an integer.

  • Big numbers → positive $n$ (move the point left)
  • Small numbers → negative $n$ (move the point right)

Multiplying: multiply the numbers, add the indices.

Dividing: divide the numbers, subtract the indices.

📝 Worked example

Write 0.000472 in scientific notation.

Move the point 4 places right: $4.72$

Since we moved right, $n=-4$.

Answer = $4.72\times10^{-4}$

🚨 Exam trap

Writing $47.2\times10^{-5}$ — the first number must be between 1 and 10. Also mixing up the direction of the point movement.

Practice

1

Calculate $(3\times10^{5})\times(4\times10^{-2})$, giving your answer in scientific notation.

Show answer

$3\times4=12$, indices $5+(-2)=3$, so $12\times10^{3}=1.2\times10^{4}$

2

Write 6,850,000 in scientific notation.

Show answer

$6.85\times10^{6}$

4Expanding brackets

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Expanding Brackets | National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

Multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second.

Single bracket: $a(b+c)=ab+ac$

Double bracket: $(a+b)(c+d)=ac+ad+bc+bd$

Squared bracket: $(x+3)^2=(x+3)(x+3)$ — not $x^2+9$.

📝 Worked example

Expand $(2x-3)(x+5)$.

$2x(x)+2x(5)+(-3)(x)+(-3)(5)$

$=2x^2+10x-3x-15$

$=2x^2+7x-15$

🚨 Exam trap

Squaring each term separately: $(x+3)^2 \ne x^2+9$. Also losing a negative sign when multiplying a negative by a negative.

Practice

1

Expand $(x+4)(x-7)$

Show answer

$x^2-7x+4x-28 = x^2-3x-28$

2

Expand $(3a-2)^2$

Show answer

$(3a-2)(3a-2)=9a^2-6a-6a+4=9a^2-12a+4$

5Factorising

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Factorising | National 5 Maths 2022 Exams

💡 The rule

Common factor: take out the highest common factor. $6x^2+9x=3x(2x+3)$.

Difference of two squares: $a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)$.

Trinomial ($x^2+bx+c$): find two numbers that multiply to $c$ and add to $b$.

Trinomial ($ax^2+bx+c$ with $a\ne1$): find two numbers multiplying to $ac$ and adding to $b$, then split the middle term.

📝 Worked example

Factorise $x^2-5x-24$.

Two numbers multiplying to $-24$ and adding to $-5$: $-8$ and $+3$.

$=(x-8)(x+3)$

Check by expanding: $x^2+3x-8x-24=x^2-5x-24$ ✓

🚨 Exam trap

Getting the signs wrong in the brackets. Always expand your answer back to check. Also forgetting to take out a common factor first before trying other methods.

Practice

1

Factorise $4x^2-25$

Show answer

Difference of two squares: $(2x-5)(2x+5)$

2

Factorise $2x^2+7x+3$

Show answer

$ac=6$, need two numbers multiplying to 6 and adding to 7: 6 and 1. Split: $2x^2+6x+x+3 = 2x(x+3)+1(x+3)=(2x+1)(x+3)$

6Completing the square

✋ Non-calculator

▶ National 5 Maths | Completing The Square

💡 The rule

Rewrite $x^2+bx+c$ as $(x+\tfrac{b}{2})^2 - (\tfrac{b}{2})^2 + c$.

In the form $(x+p)^2+q$:

  • $p = \dfrac{b}{2}$
  • $q = c - p^2$

The minimum turning point of the graph is $(-p,\ q)$.

📝 Worked example

Write $x^2+6x+2$ in the form $(x+p)^2+q$.

$p = 6/2 = 3$

$q = 2 - 3^2 = 2 - 9 = -7$

Answer = $(x+3)^2-7$

Minimum turning point: $(-3,\ -7)$

🚨 Exam trap

Sign error on $p$ — the bracket is $(x+p)$ so a positive $b$ gives a negative $x$-coordinate at the minimum. Also forgetting to subtract $p^2$ when finding $q$.

Practice

1

Write $x^2-8x+5$ in the form $(x+p)^2+q$.

Show answer

$p=-4$, $q=5-16=-11$, so $(x-4)^2-11$

2

State the minimum turning point of $y=x^2+10x+3$.

Show answer

$p=5$, $q=3-25=-22$, so the minimum is $(-5,\ -22)$

7Algebraic fractions

⚖️ Either paper

▶ How to Add and Subtract Algebraic Fractions | National 5

💡 The rule

Treat them exactly like number fractions.

Adding/subtracting: find a common denominator.

Multiplying: multiply across, then cancel.

Dividing: flip and multiply.

Always factorise first — cancelling only works on factors, never on terms.

📝 Worked example

Simplify $\dfrac{x^2-9}{x^2+x-12}$.

Factorise top: $(x-3)(x+3)$

Factorise bottom: $(x+4)(x-3)$

$=\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{(x+4)(x-3)} = \dfrac{x+3}{x+4}$

🚨 Exam trap

Cancelling across a $+$ sign. $\dfrac{x+3}{x+4} \ne \dfrac{3}{4}$. You may only cancel whole factors. Also, the mark scheme wants to see the factorisation before you cancel.

Practice

1

Simplify $\dfrac{2x+6}{x^2-9}$

Show answer

$\dfrac{2(x+3)}{(x-3)(x+3)} = \dfrac{2}{x-3}$

2

Express $\dfrac{3}{x}+\dfrac{2}{x+1}$ as a single fraction.

Show answer

$\dfrac{3(x+1)+2x}{x(x+1)} = \dfrac{5x+3}{x(x+1)}$

8Changing the subject

⚖️ Either paper

▶ The Whole Of CHANGING THE SUBJECT in National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

Rearrange so the required variable sits alone on one side.

1. Remove fractions by multiplying through. 2. Expand brackets if the variable appears inside them. 3. Collect all terms containing the variable on one side. 4. Factorise it out. 5. Divide by the bracket.

If the subject appears twice, you must factorise.

📝 Worked example

Make $x$ the subject of $y=\dfrac{3x+2}{x-1}$.

Multiply through: $y(x-1)=3x+2$

Expand: $yx-y=3x+2$

Collect $x$ terms: $yx-3x=2+y$

Factorise: $x(y-3)=2+y$

$x=\dfrac{2+y}{y-3}$

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting to factorise when the subject appears twice — you cannot just divide one term. Also dropping signs when moving terms across the equals sign.

Practice

1

Make $r$ the subject of $C=2\pi r$

Show answer

$r=\dfrac{C}{2\pi}$

2

Make $a$ the subject of $v^2=u^2+2as$

Show answer

$2as=v^2-u^2$, so $a=\dfrac{v^2-u^2}{2s}$

9Equations & inequations

⚖️ Either paper

▶ The Whole Of Solving Inequations In National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

Linear equations: do the same operation to both sides until the variable is alone.

Equations with fractions: multiply every term by the common denominator.

Inequations: solve exactly like an equation, but reverse the inequality sign when you multiply or divide by a negative number.

Answers to inequations are shown on a number line: open circle for $<$ or $>$, closed circle for $\le$ or $\ge$.

📝 Worked example

Solve $3(x-2) < 5x+4$.

Expand: $3x-6 < 5x+4$

Subtract $3x$: $-6 < 2x+4$

Subtract 4: $-10 < 2x$

Divide by 2: $-5 < x$, i.e. $x > -5$

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting to flip the sign when dividing by a negative. Also forgetting to multiply every term by the denominator when clearing fractions.

Practice

1

Solve $4x-7 = 2x+9$

Show answer

$2x=16$, so $x=8$

2

Solve $-2x \ge 8$ and show the answer on a number line.

Show answer

Divide by $-2$ and flip: $x \le -4$. Closed circle at $-4$, arrow to the left.

10Simultaneous equations

⚖️ Either paper

▶ The Whole Of SIMULTANEOUS EQUATIONS in National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

Elimination method: 1. Scale one or both equations so the coefficients of one variable match. 2. Add or subtract to eliminate that variable. 3. Solve for the remaining variable. 4. Substitute back to find the other.

Graphical method: the solution is where the two lines cross.

Check by substituting both values into both original equations.

📝 Worked example

Solve $3x+2y=12$ and $x-2y=4$.

Add the equations (the $2y$ terms cancel):

$4x=16 \Rightarrow x=4$

Substitute into $x-2y=4$: $4-2y=4 \Rightarrow y=0$

Solution: $x=4,\ y=0$

🚨 Exam trap

Substituting back into only one equation. Also sign errors when subtracting equations — subtracting is the same as adding the negative.

Practice

1

Solve $2x+y=11$ and $3x-y=4$.

Show answer

Add: $5x=15$, so $x=3$. Then $6+y=11$, so $y=5$. Solution $(3,5)$.

2

Solve $4x+3y=18$ and $2x-3y=6$.

Show answer

Add: $6x=24$, so $x=4$. Then $16+3y=18$, so $3y=2$, $y=\tfrac23$. Solution $(4,\tfrac23)$.

11The straight line

⚖️ Either paper

▶ The Whole Of Straight Line In National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

$y=mx+c$ where $m$ is the gradient and $c$ is the y-intercept.

Gradient from two points: $m=\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$

Distance between two points: $d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$

Midpoint: $\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$

Parallel lines have equal gradients. Perpendicular lines have gradients multiplying to $-1$.

📝 Worked example

Find the equation of the line through $(2,5)$ and $(6,13)$.

$m=\dfrac{13-5}{6-2}=\dfrac{8}{4}=2$

Using $(2,5)$: $5=2(2)+c \Rightarrow c=1$

$y=2x+1$

🚨 Exam trap

Subtracting the coordinates in the wrong order — keep the same order top and bottom. Also, perpendicular gradient is the negative reciprocal ($-\tfrac{1}{m}$), not just $-m$.

Practice

1

Find the gradient of the line through $(1,2)$ and $(4,11)$.

Show answer

$m=\dfrac{11-2}{4-1}=\dfrac{9}{3}=3$

2

Find the equation of the line perpendicular to $y=3x+2$ passing through $(6,1)$.

Show answer

Perpendicular gradient $=-\tfrac13$. $1=-\tfrac13(6)+c \Rightarrow 1=-2+c \Rightarrow c=3$. So $y=-\tfrac13x+3$.

12Function notation

⚖️ Either paper

▶ How to Master FUNCTIONS | National 5

💡 The rule

$f(x)$ means "the function $f$ applied to $x$".

  • $f(3)$ = substitute $x=3$.
  • $f(a)=b$ means solve for $a$.
  • Composite functions: $f(g(x))$ means apply $g$ first, then $f$.
  • Inverse: find $f^{-1}(x)$ by swapping $x$ and $y$, then rearranging.

📝 Worked example

$f(x)=3x-4$ and $g(x)=x^2$.

Find $f(g(2))$.

$g(2)=2^2=4$

$f(4)=3(4)-4=8$

$f(g(2))=8$

🚨 Exam trap

Applying the functions in the wrong order — $f(g(x))$ is not the same as $g(f(x))$. Also, confusing $f^{-1}(x)$ with $\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$.

Practice

1

$f(x)=2x+1$. Find $f^{-1}(x)$.

Show answer

$y=2x+1 \Rightarrow x=\dfrac{y-1}{2}$, so $f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{x-1}{2}$

2

$f(x)=x^2-5$. Solve $f(x)=20$.

Show answer

$x^2-5=20 \Rightarrow x^2=25 \Rightarrow x=\pm5$

13Quadratic graphs & equations

⚖️ Either paper

▶ The Whole Of Quadratics In National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

A quadratic has the form $y=ax^2+bx+c$ and graphs as a parabola.

  • $a>0$ → opens upwards (minimum)
  • $a<0$ → opens downwards (maximum)

Roots = where $y=0$ (where it crosses the x-axis).

Solving by factorising: rearrange to $ax^2+bx+c=0$, factorise, then set each bracket to zero.

Quadratic formula: $x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$

Discriminant $b^2-4ac$: $>0$ two roots, $=0$ one root, $<0$ no real roots.

📝 Worked example

Solve $x^2-3x-10=0$.

Factorise: $(x-5)(x+2)=0$

So $x-5=0$ or $x+2=0$

$x=5$ or $x=-2$

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting that a quadratic usually has two solutions. Also, not rearranging to $=0$ before factorising — you cannot factorise $x^2=3x+10$ without moving everything first.

Practice

1

Solve $2x^2+5x-3=0$

Show answer

$ac=-6$; numbers multiplying to $-6$ adding to $5$: $6,-1$. Split: $2x^2+6x-x-3=2x(x+3)-1(x+3)=(2x-1)(x+3)$. So $x=\tfrac12$ or $x=-3$.

2

State the number of real roots of $x^2+4x+9=0$.

Show answer

$b^2-4ac=16-36=-20<0$, so no real roots.

14Pythagoras (incl. 3D & converse)

⚖️ Either paper

▶ All Of Pythagoras In National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

In a right-angled triangle: $a^2+b^2=c^2$ where $c$ is the hypotenuse (the longest side, opposite the right angle).

Finding the hypotenuse: add the squares, then square root.

Finding a shorter side: subtract the squares, then square root.

Converse: if $a^2+b^2=c^2$ for a triangle's three sides, the triangle is right-angled. If $a^2+b^2 \ne c^2$, it is not.

3D problems: find a diagonal in a face first, then use it as a side in the cross-section triangle.

📝 Worked example

A cuboid measures 3 cm × 4 cm × 12 cm. Find the space diagonal.

Face diagonal: $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=5$ cm

Space diagonal: $\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{25+144}=\sqrt{169}$

= 13 cm

🚨 Exam trap

Adding the squares when you need a shorter side. The hypotenuse is always the side opposite the right angle — identify it first. In the converse, you must compare the two shorter sides' squares with the longest side.

Practice

1

A triangle has sides 9 cm, 12 cm and 15 cm. Is it right-angled?

Show answer

$9^2+12^2=81+144=225$ and $15^2=225$. They are equal, so by the converse of Pythagoras the triangle is right-angled (right angle opposite the 15 cm side).

2

A ladder 5 m long leans against a wall, with its foot 3 m from the base. How far up the wall does it reach?

Show answer

$h=\sqrt{5^2-3^2}=\sqrt{25-9}=\sqrt{16}=4$ m

15Arcs & sectors

🧮 Calculator

▶ All Of Arcs And Sectors In National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

The arc length and sector area are fractions of the whole circle's circumference/area, given by $\dfrac{\text{angle}}{360}$.

Arc length: $\dfrac{\theta}{360}\times \pi d$

Sector area: $\dfrac{\theta}{360}\times \pi r^2$

Chord length (from a triangle): use the cosine rule with the two radii.

Angles may be given in radians — then use $\ell=r\theta$ and $A=\tfrac12 r^2\theta$.

📝 Worked example

Find the area of a sector of radius 8 cm with angle 135°.

$\dfrac{135}{360}\times\pi\times 8^2 = 0.375\times\pi\times64$

$=75.4$ cm² (1 d.p.)

🚨 Exam trap

Using the diameter in the area formula, or the radius in the circumference formula. Also forgetting the answer is a fraction of the full circle — check it is smaller than the whole area.

Practice

1

Find the arc length of a sector of radius 10 cm with angle 72°.

Show answer

$\dfrac{72}{360}\times\pi\times20 = 0.2\times\pi\times20 = 4\pi \approx 12.6$ cm

2

A sector has radius 6 cm and area 18 cm². Find its angle.

Show answer

$\dfrac{\theta}{360}\times\pi\times36=18 \Rightarrow \theta\pi=180 \Rightarrow \theta=\dfrac{180}{\pi}\approx57.3°$

16Circle angle properties

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Circles and Angle Properties National 5

💡 The rule

The circle theorems you must know:

  • The angle in a semicircle is 90°.
  • A tangent meets a radius at 90°.
  • The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it.
  • Angles in the same segment are equal.
  • The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference (standing on the same arc).
  • Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°.
  • A radius bisects the angle between two tangents from the same external point.

📝 Worked example

Angle $ABC$ = 35° where $AC$ is a diameter. Find angle $ACB$.

The angle in a semicircle is 90°, so angle $ABC \times ...$ — here angle $ABC$ is at the circumference subtending the diameter, so angle $ABC=90°$ only if $B$ is on the circle with $AC$ as diameter.

Using the triangle: angles sum to 180°, so $90°+35°+\angle ACB=180°$

$\angle ACB = 55°$

🚨 Exam trap

Quoting a theorem without stating why it applies — the reason earns marks in proof-style questions. Also, reflex vs non-reflex angles at the centre are a classic trap: 'twice the angle at the circumference' refers to the angle standing on the same arc.

Practice

1

Two tangents are drawn to a circle from point P. The angle between them is 68°. Find the angle at the centre between the two radii.

Show answer

The radii meet the tangents at 90°. In quadrilateral OAPB: $90+90+68+\theta=360$, so $\theta=112°$.

2

A cyclic quadrilateral has angles $2x$, $x+30$, $3x$ and $x+50$ degrees. Find $x$.

Show answer

Opposite angles sum to 180°. Also all four sum to 360: $2x+x+30+3x+x+50=360 \Rightarrow 7x+80=360 \Rightarrow x=40$.

17Similar shapes

⚖️ Either paper

▶ How to Solve Similar Shapes, Area & Volume. National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

Similar shapes have the same shape but different size. All corresponding lengths scale by the linear scale factor $k$.

  • Lengths $\times k$
  • Areas $\times k^2$
  • Volumes $\times k^3$

So: $k=\dfrac{\text{new length}}{\text{old length}}$

Always find $k$ from the lengths first.

📝 Worked example

Two similar bottles have heights 12 cm and 8 cm. The large bottle holds 540 ml. Find the small bottle's volume.

$k=\dfrac{8}{12}=\dfrac{2}{3}$

Volume factor $=k^3=\left(\dfrac23\right)^3=\dfrac{8}{27}$

$V=540\times\dfrac{8}{27}=160$ ml

= 160 ml

🚨 Exam trap

Using the linear scale factor on an area or volume. Volume scales by the cube of the length factor. Give $k$ as an exact fraction (e.g. $\tfrac23$) rather than a rounded decimal — rounding $0.67$ can lose a mark.

Practice

1

Two similar cones have base radii 3 cm and 5 cm. Find the ratio of their volumes.

Show answer

Volume ratio $=3^3:5^3=27:125$

2

Two similar rectangles have areas $24\text{ cm}^2$ and $54\text{ cm}^2$. The smaller is 4 cm long. Find the corresponding length on the larger.

Show answer

Area factor $=\dfrac{54}{24}=\dfrac94$, so $k=\sqrt{\dfrac94}=\dfrac32$. Length $=4\times\dfrac32=6$ cm.

18Trigonometry & bearings

⚖️ Either paper

▶ All Of Bearings In National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

SOHCAHTOA for right-angled triangles:

  • $\sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}$,$\cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}$,$\tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$

Bearings are measured clockwise from North, always written as three digits (e.g. 047°).

  • Back-bearing $=$ forward bearing $\pm 180°$.
  • Bearings are drawn from the North line at each point.

📝 Worked example

A ship sails 6 km on a bearing of 090°, then 8 km on a bearing of 180°. How far is it from the start?

These directions are perpendicular, so use Pythagoras:

$d=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}$

= 10 km

🚨 Exam trap

Measuring bearings anticlockwise, or from the wrong point. Also, using the same North line for both legs of a journey — each point has its own North line. Give bearings as 3 digits (e.g. 060°, not 60°).

Practice

1

A boat sails 5 km on a bearing of 060°. How far East of its start is it?

Show answer

East component $=5\sin60° = 5\times0.866 = 4.33$ km

2

The bearing of B from A is 125°. Find the bearing of A from B.

Show answer

$125+180=305°$

19Sine rule, cosine rule & triangle area

⚖️ Either paper

▶ COSINE RULE for an ANGLE or A SIDE - National 5 Maths

💡 The rule

For any triangle:

Sine rule (sides): $\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}$ — use when you have a matching side-and-angle pair.

Cosine rule (side): $a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A$ — use when you have two sides and the included angle.

Cosine rule (angle): $\cos A=\dfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}$ — use when you have all three sides.

Area: $A=\tfrac12 ab\sin C$

For the sine rule finding an angle, check whether the answer could be the obtuse supplement.

📝 Worked example

In triangle $ABC$, $b=7$, $c=9$ and angle $A=52°$. Find $a$.

$a^2=7^2+9^2-2(7)(9)\cos52°$

$=49+81-126(0.6157)$

$=130-77.6=52.4$

$a=\sqrt{52.4}=7.2$ (1 d.p.)

🚨 Exam trap

Using the sine rule when the cosine rule is needed (no matching pair). Also forgetting the $2bc\cos A$ term is subtracted. When finding an angle with the sine rule, remember $\sin^{-1}$ only gives the acute angle.

Practice

1

Find the area of a triangle with sides 8 cm and 11 cm including an angle of 40°.

Show answer

$A=\tfrac12(8)(11)\sin40° = 44\times0.6428 = 28.3\text{ cm}^2$

2

A triangle has sides 5, 7 and 9. Find the largest angle.

Show answer

Largest angle is opposite 9. $\cos C=\dfrac{25+49-81}{2(5)(7)}=\dfrac{-7}{70}=-0.1$, so $C=\cos^{-1}(-0.1)=95.7°$

20Vectors

⚖️ Either paper

▶ All Of Vectors In National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

A vector has magnitude and direction. Written as a column: $\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}$ (2D) or $\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{pmatrix}$ (3D).

Magnitude: $|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ (add $c^2$ in 3D).

Addition/subtraction: add or subtract corresponding components.

Scalar multiplication: multiply each component by the number.

Pathways: if you can walk from one point to another, the vectors add — including reversing $\overrightarrow{AB}=-\overrightarrow{BA}$.

📝 Worked example

$\mathbf{u}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\-2\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\5\end{pmatrix}$.

Find $|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|$.

$\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}$

$|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{2^2+3^2}=\sqrt{13}$

$=\sqrt{13}$ (≈ 3.61)

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting that $\overrightarrow{BA}$ is the negative of $\overrightarrow{AB}$. Also, magnitude must always be positive, and leaving it as an unsimplified surd when the question asks for the simplest form.

Practice

1

Find the magnitude of $\begin{pmatrix}4\\-5\\7\end{pmatrix}$.

Show answer

$\sqrt{16+25+49}=\sqrt{90}=3\sqrt{10}$

2

$\overrightarrow{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}$ and $\overrightarrow{BC}=\begin{pmatrix}-5\\1\end{pmatrix}$. Find $\overrightarrow{AC}$.

Show answer

$\overrightarrow{AC}=\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{BC}=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\4\end{pmatrix}$

21Volume & surface area

⚖️ Either paper

▶ All Of Volume In National 5 Maths!

💡 The rule

Prism: area of cross-section × length

Cylinder: $V=\pi r^2h$

Cone: $V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h$

Sphere: $V=\tfrac43\pi r^3$

Composite solids: split into simple shapes, add or subtract.

For surface area of a curved solid, make sure you include the flat faces.

📝 Worked example

A solid consists of a cylinder (radius 3 cm, height 10 cm) with a hemisphere on top. Find the total volume.

Cylinder: $\pi(3)^2(10)=90\pi$

Hemisphere: $\tfrac12\times\tfrac43\pi(3)^3=18\pi$

Total $=108\pi \approx 339.3\text{ cm}^3$

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting the $\tfrac13$ for a cone or the $\tfrac43$ for a sphere. Also using the diameter instead of the radius — the most common error in this topic. For a hemisphere, remember to halve the sphere's volume.

Practice

1

Find the volume of a sphere of diameter 12 cm, to 1 d.p.

Show answer

$r=6$. $V=\tfrac43\pi(6)^3=\tfrac43\pi(216)=288\pi\approx904.8\text{ cm}^3$

2

A cone has radius 5 cm and height 12 cm. Find its slant height and curved surface area.

Show answer

Slant $l=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{169}=13$ cm. Curved SA $=\pi r l=\pi(5)(13)=65\pi\approx204.2\text{ cm}^2$

22Statistics & standard deviation

🧮 Calculator

▶ Calculating Standard Deviation | N5 Maths/Apps

💡 The rule

Mean: $\bar{x}=\dfrac{\sum x}{n}$

Median: the middle value (average the two middle values if $n$ is even).

Quartiles: Q1 = median of lower half, Q3 = median of upper half.

IQR $=$ Q3 − Q1 (measures spread, ignores outliers).

Semi-interquartile range $=\dfrac{\text{IQR}}{2}$

Standard deviation: $s=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum(x-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}}$ — measures how spread out values are.

Box plots: show min, Q1, median, Q3, max. Comparing distributions requires a comment on both the median (typical value) and the spread (IQR/SD).

📝 Worked example

Find the standard deviation of 4, 7, 8, 9, 12.

$\bar{x}=\dfrac{40}{5}=8$

Deviations: $-4,-1,0,1,4$; squares: $16,1,0,1,16$; sum $=34$

$s=\sqrt{\dfrac{34}{5-1}}=\sqrt{8.5}=2.92$

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting to order the data first before finding the median or quartiles. When comparing two distributions, a comment on only one of median/spread loses the comparison mark — you must say something about both, and mention which is larger.

Practice

1

Find the median and IQR of: 5, 8, 2, 11, 4, 7, 9, 3

Show answer

Ordered: 2,3,4,5,7,8,9,11. Median $=\dfrac{5+7}{2}=6$. Q1$=\dfrac{3+4}{2}=3.5$, Q3$=\dfrac{8+9}{2}=8.5$. IQR$=5$.

2

Two classes' test scores have median 62, IQR 14 and median 58, IQR 22. Compare.

Show answer

Class 1 scored higher on average (62 > 58). Class 2's scores were more varied (IQR 22 > 14).

23Probability

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Apps - Probability 1

💡 The rule

$P(E)=\dfrac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$

All probabilities lie between 0 and 1. The probabilities of all outcomes sum to 1.

Complement: $P(\text{not }E)=1-P(E)$

Expected frequency: $P(E)\times\text{number of trials}$

Independent events (both happen): multiply.

Mutually exclusive (one or the other): add.

📝 Worked example

A bag has 5 red and 3 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement. Find P(both red).

$P(\text{1st red})=\dfrac58$

After taking one red, 4 red remain from 7:

$P(\text{2nd red})=\dfrac47$

$P=\dfrac58\times\dfrac47=\dfrac{20}{56}=\dfrac{5}{14}$

🚨 Exam trap

Using 'with replacement' probabilities when the question says 'without replacement' — the denominator changes. Also, adding when you should multiply (independent events use multiplication).

Practice

1

A fair die is rolled. Find the probability of rolling a number greater than 4.

Show answer

Favourable: 5, 6 → $\dfrac26=\dfrac13$

2

The probability it rains is 0.3. Find the expected number of rainy days in a 30-day month.

Show answer

$0.3\times30=9$ days

24Tolerance & bounds

✋ Non-calculator

▶ Solving Limits of Accuracy Problems - Corbettmaths

💡 The rule

A tolerance is the permitted variation around a stated value.

If a value $v$ is given with tolerance $\pm t$, then:

  • minimum $=v-t$
  • maximum $=v+t$

The value is acceptable if it lies between (and including) these limits.

For a stated upper limit $U$ and lower limit $L$, the tolerance is $\pm\dfrac{U-L}{2}$.

📝 Worked example

A metal rod is specified as 250 mm long with a tolerance of ±0.5 mm. A rod measures 250.7 mm. Is it acceptable?

Lower limit $=249.5$ mm, upper limit $=250.5$ mm

$250.7 > 250.5$, so it is not acceptable.

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting the limits are inclusive — a value exactly on the limit IS acceptable. Also, tolerance written as a ± value must be halved if you are given a range, and added to/subtracted from the nominal value only once.

Practice

1

A packet must contain 500 g ± 15 g. State the acceptable range.

Show answer

485 g to 515 g inclusive

2

A bolt has limits 39.8 mm and 40.2 mm. Express the tolerance as a ± value about the nominal size.

Show answer

Nominal $=\dfrac{39.8+40.2}{2}=40$ mm; tolerance $=\pm0.2$ mm

25Ratio & proportion

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Applications of Maths: Ratio Calculations

💡 The rule

Simplifying ratios: divide all parts by their highest common factor.

Sharing in a ratio: total parts → find the value of one part → multiply.

Direct proportion: as one quantity increases, the other increases at the same rate. Use $y=kx$.

Inverse proportion: as one increases, the other decreases. Use $y=\dfrac{k}{x}$. Find $k$ first, then use it.

📝 Worked example

£240 is shared between Ali and Ben in the ratio 3:5.

Total parts $=3+5=8$

One part $=\pounds240\div8=\pounds30$

Ali: $3\times30=\pounds90$; Ben: $5\times30=\pounds150$

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting to find the value of one part first. For inverse proportion, a common error is multiplying instead of dividing — always find the constant $k$ first.

Practice

1

Simplify the ratio 24:36:60

Show answer

HCF is 12, so $2:3:5$

2

$y$ is inversely proportional to $x$. When $x=4$, $y=15$. Find $y$ when $x=10$.

Show answer

$k=xy=60$, so $y=\dfrac{60}{10}=6$

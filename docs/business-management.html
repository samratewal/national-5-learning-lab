1Role of business in society

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Business Management Lesson 1 Business Basics

💡 The rule

Businesses exist to satisfy needs and wants by producing goods and providing services.

Their role in society:

  • Create wealth and employment
  • Provide goods and services consumers want
  • Pay taxes that fund public services
  • Drive innovation and improve living standards
  • Provide choice through competition

Sectors of industry: primary (extracting raw materials), secondary (manufacturing), tertiary (providing services).

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain two ways businesses benefit society. (4)

Model answer: Businesses create employment (1), which gives people income to spend and improves their standard of living (2). Businesses also pay taxes such as corporation tax (1), which the government uses to fund public services like the NHS and schools, benefiting everyone (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Giving a list of points with no development. Every mark needs a 'which means that...' SQA marks are awarded for the consequence of the point, not for naming it.

Practice

1

Explain one way businesses provide choice to consumers. (2)

Show answer

Competition between businesses means firms offer different products and prices (1). This gives consumers options so they can pick the product best suited to their needs and budget (2).

2

Describe the difference between a need and a want, with an example. (2)

Show answer

A need is essential for survival, e.g. food, shelter, clothing (1). A want is desirable but not essential, e.g. a games console or a takeaway meal (2).

2Factors of production

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Business Management Lesson 2 Factors of Production and Entrepreneurs

💡 The rule

Four factors of production are needed to make goods and services:

  • Land — natural resources (raw materials, water, minerals)
  • Labour — the workforce
  • Capital — machinery, equipment, money invested
  • Enterprise — the entrepreneur who takes the risk and organises the other three

Rewards: land → rent, labour → wages, capital → interest, enterprise → profit.

📝 Worked example

A bakery needs:

  • Land: the plot and flour from wheat
  • Labour: bakers and counter staff
  • Capital: industrial ovens, mixers, delivery van
  • Enterprise: the owner who set it up and takes the financial risk

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing capital with 'money in the bank' only. In business, capital means the tools, machinery and equipment used in production as well as finance. Also leaving out enterprise, which is the factor that organises all the others.

Practice

1

State the reward for each factor of production. (4)

Show answer

Land → rent; labour → wages/salaries; capital → interest; enterprise → profit.

2

Explain why enterprise is essential to a new business. (2)

Show answer

An entrepreneur takes the financial risk of starting the business and organises the other factors of production (1). Without someone willing to take that risk and invest, the business would not exist (2).

3Sectors of industry & economy

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Sectors of Industry - SQA National 5 Business

💡 The rule

Sectors of industry:

  • Primary — extracting raw materials (farming, mining, fishing)
  • Secondary — manufacturing and construction (car plants, bakeries)
  • Tertiary — providing services (retail, banking, hairdressing)

Sectors of the economy:

  • Private — owned by individuals, profit-driven (Tesco)
  • Public — owned by government, funded by taxes (NHS, schools)
  • Third — non-profit, aims to help (charities, social enterprises)

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain the difference between the private sector and the third sector. (2)

Model answer: The private sector is owned by individuals or shareholders and exists to make a profit for its owners (1). The third sector, such as charities and social enterprises, aims to help a social cause and reinvests any surplus rather than distributing profit to owners (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Mixing up sectors of industry (primary/secondary/tertiary) with sectors of the economy (private/public/third) — they are different classifications. Also, describing the third sector as 'no money involved' — social enterprises can and do make money, they just do not distribute profit to owners.

Practice

1

Classify these by sector of industry: a fishing boat, a car factory, a hotel. (3)

Show answer

Fishing boat = primary; car factory = secondary; hotel = tertiary.

2

Explain why the public sector is funded differently from the private sector. (2)

Show answer

The public sector is funded by taxation and government budgets (1) because it provides services for the whole population and is not run to make a profit (2).

4Stakeholders

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 and Higher Business Management - STAKEHOLDERS

💡 The rule

A stakeholder is anyone with an interest in a business.

  • Owners/shareholders — profit, dividends
  • Employees — pay, job security, working conditions
  • Customers — quality, price, service
  • Suppliers — prompt payment, repeat orders
  • Banks — loan repayment, interest
  • Local community — jobs, environment, noise
  • Government — tax revenue, compliance, employment

Stakeholders can influence the business and be impacted by it.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain the impact on employees if a factory closes. (2)

Model answer: Employees would lose their income and face redundancy (1). This would affect their ability to pay bills and support their families, and finding new work in a small town could be very difficult (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Listing stakeholders without saying how they are affected. The marks come from the impact, not the label. Also, forgetting that stakeholders can have conflicting interests — employees want higher wages while owners want lower costs.

Practice

1

Explain how shareholders can influence a business. (2)

Show answer

Shareholders own part of the company and vote on major decisions at general meetings (1). If they are unhappy with performance they can vote out directors or sell their shares, which can lower the share price (2).

2

Describe one conflict between customers and shareholders. (2)

Show answer

Customers want lower prices and higher quality (1), but shareholders want higher profits, which may mean cutting costs or raising prices — the two aims directly conflict (2).

5Internal & external environments

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Business Management Lesson 4 Types of Ownership Private Sector

💡 The rule

Internal environment — factors the business controls:

  • Finance, employees, current technology, organisational structure, company culture

External environment — factors outside the business's control (PESTEC):

  • Political — government policy, tax, Brexit
  • Economic — interest rates, inflation, exchange rates
  • Social — demographic change, lifestyle trends, ethics
  • Technological — automation, e-commerce, AI
  • Environmental — climate, sustainability pressure
  • Competitive — rival businesses, market share

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how an increase in interest rates affects a small business. (2)

Model answer: If interest rates rise, repayments on the business's existing loans increase (1), raising its costs and reducing profit. It also makes new borrowing more expensive and consumers more cautious about spending (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Naming PESTEC factors without explaining their effect on the business. Also, listing internal factors as external — remember internal means the business controls it directly.

Practice

1

Give two political factors affecting a UK retailer. (2)

Show answer

Changes in corporation tax affect profits; changes to import tariffs or trading rules (e.g. post-Brexit) affect the cost of goods brought in from abroad.

2

Explain how a technological change could threaten a business. (2)

Show answer

If competitors adopt new technology such as online ordering, they may offer faster or cheaper service (1). A business that does not keep up loses customers to those competitors (2).

6The marketing mix

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Marketing Mix - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

The 4 Ps of the marketing mix:

  • Product — what is sold, its features and quality
  • Price — how much is charged (and pricing strategy)
  • Place — where and how it reaches customers (shops, online)
  • Promotion — how customers are made aware (advertising, offers)

The mix must be consistent — a luxury product needs a high price and premium promotion, not a bargain price and a discount flyer.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how Nokia's failure to adapt its product affected its market share. (2)

Model answer: Nokia did not develop its product quickly enough when touchscreen smartphones arrived (1). Competitors such as Apple offered features customers wanted, so Nokia lost customers and its market share collapsed (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Describing the 4 Ps without linking them to the specific business in the case study. Also failing to explain how the elements work together — examiners often ask for the benefit of an integrated mix.

Practice

1

Explain why a consistent marketing mix matters for a premium brand. (2)

Show answer

All four Ps should signal the same quality message (1). A high price, quality product, exclusive place and prestigious promotion together create a premium image — discounting would damage it (2).

2

Describe one benefit of online selling under 'place'. (2)

Show answer

An online store lets a business reach customers far beyond its local area (1), increasing potential sales without the cost of opening physical shops (2).

7Market research

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5, Int 2 and Higher Business Management: Market Research

💡 The rule

Market research gathers information about customers, competitors and the market.

Desk research (secondary) — data that already exists: internet, newspapers, government reports, sales records. Cheaper and faster, but may be out of date or not specific to the business.

Field research (primary) — new data the business collects: surveys, questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation, trials. More specific and up to date, but slower and more expensive.

Quantitative data = numbers. Qualitative data = opinions and feelings.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a questionnaire. (4)

Model answer: A questionnaire can gather information from many people quickly and cheaply (1), giving a statistically useful picture of customer opinion (2). However, people may not answer honestly or may skip questions (1), so results can be inaccurate and the business may make a wrong decision (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Saying field research is 'better' without saying why. Also, confusing desk and field — desk = already exists, field = you collect it yourself.

Practice

1

Give two examples of desk research a café could use. (2)

Show answer

Looking at competitors' prices online; reading local council population and spending reports.

2

Explain why a focus group is useful for testing a new product. (2)

Show answer

A focus group gives detailed opinions and lets the business ask follow-up questions (1), so it can understand why people like or dislike a product and improve it before launch (2).

8Market segmentation

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Market Segmentation - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

Segmenting means dividing a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics, so marketing can be targeted.

Common ways to segment:

  • Age — children, teenagers, adults, older people
  • Gender — male/female focused products
  • Income — budget vs luxury
  • Location — local, national, rural/urban
  • Lifestyle — health-conscious, sporty, eco-minded

A target market is the segment the business aims its product at.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how a business could use age to segment a market. (2)

Model answer: The business could identify that teenagers are the main users of its product (1). It could then focus advertising on social media platforms those teenagers use, rather than expensive television advertising (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Describing a segment without explaining the marketing benefit of targeting it. Also, assuming one segment is the whole market — businesses often target several.

Practice

1

Describe two ways a gym could segment its market. (2)

Show answer

By lifestyle — serious athletes versus casual exercisers; by age — students versus older adults wanting low-impact exercise.

2

Explain one benefit of segmentation. (2)

Show answer

Segmenting lets a business direct its marketing effort at the people most likely to buy (1), which means less money wasted on advertising to people who are not interested and more effective sales (2).

9Product life cycle

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 and Higher Business Management: Product Life Cycle and Extension Strategies

💡 The rule

Four stages: Development → Introduction → Growth → Maturity → Decline.

  • Introduction: low sales, high promotion costs, often a loss
  • Growth: sales rise quickly, competitors enter
  • Maturity: sales peak, competition fierce, profits squeezed
  • Decline: sales fall, business must decide to update, discount or withdraw

Strategies to extend the cycle: product development, new markets, rebranding, price cuts.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain the strategy a business might use when a product reaches maturity. (2)

Model answer: When sales peak in maturity, the business might extend the product's life by updating it, for example adding new features or new flavours (1). This can attract existing customers to buy again and delay the decline in sales (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Getting the stages out of order or confusing maturity with decline — maturity is the peak, decline is after it. Also, describing the stages without stating the business action for each.

Practice

1

State what happens to profits in the introduction stage and why. (2)

Show answer

Profits are usually negative or low. This is because heavy spending on promotion is needed to make customers aware of the product, but sales volumes are still small.

2

Give two ways a business can extend a product's life cycle. (2)

Show answer

Update or improve the product to attract renewed interest; find new markets or new uses for the product.

10Branding & promotion

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Branding - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a product and distinguishes it from competitors.

Benefits of branding: customer loyalty, ability to charge a higher price, easier product launches, protection from competition.

Promotion methods:

  • Advertising — TV, radio, billboard, online, social media
  • Sales promotion — discounts, buy-one-get-one-free, competitions, loyalty schemes
  • Personal selling — shop staff, sales representatives
  • Public relations (PR) — press releases, sponsorship, social media posts
  • Direct marketing — emails, texts, mailshots

Choose promotion by cost, target market and reach.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how a strong brand allows a business to charge higher prices. (2)

Model answer: A trusted brand means customers believe the product is higher quality and are willing to pay more for it (1). It also reduces the risk of switching to an unknown brand, so customers stay loyal even when cheaper alternatives exist (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Listing promotion methods without evaluating them in the case-study context. The best answers say why a specific method suits that business and its target market.

Practice

1

Explain one advantage of social media promotion over television advertising. (2)

Show answer

Social media is far cheaper and lets a business target a specific group by age and interest (1). It can also reach young customers who watch less television, so the money is used more effectively (2).

2

Give two benefits of a loyalty scheme. (2)

Show answer

It encourages repeat custom from existing customers; it builds a database of customer data that can be used for targeted marketing.

11Pricing & distribution

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Higher, Int 2 and National 5 Business Management: Pricing Strategies

💡 The rule

Pricing strategies:

  • Skimming — high initial price, lowered later (new technology)
  • Penetration — low price to win market share quickly
  • Competitive — match or beat rivals' prices
  • Cost-plus — cost of production plus a mark-up
  • Premium — high price to signal quality
  • Loss leader — sold below cost to attract customers who buy other items

Factors: production costs, competition, target market, economy, brand strength.

Distribution is how the product reaches the customer: direct (online/shop), retailer, wholesaler, or agent.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of penetration pricing. (4)

Model answer: Penetration pricing sets a low price to attract customers quickly (1), which can build market share and win loyalty before competitors respond (2). However, the low price means low or negative profit margins (1), and customers may see the product as cheap, making it hard to raise the price later (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing skimming and penetration — skimming starts high and falls; penetration starts low. Also, recommending a pricing strategy without reference to the business's costs or target market.

Practice

1

Which pricing strategy suits a brand-new budget phone? Justify. (2)

Show answer

Penetration pricing. It makes the phone attractive to price-sensitive customers, winning market share quickly against established brands.

2

Explain the difference between selling via a wholesaler and selling directly online. (2)

Show answer

A wholesaler buys in bulk and distributes to retailers, so the business loses some profit per unit but reaches many shops. Selling direct online keeps more profit per sale but the business must generate its own customers and handle delivery.

12Suppliers & supply chain

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Suppliers - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

Factors when choosing a supplier:

  • Price — cost of materials
  • Quality — reliability of the materials
  • Reliability — consistently delivering on time
  • Delivery — speed and lead time
  • Payment terms — credit offered
  • Ethical/environmental — sustainability record
  • Location — local suppliers reduce transport costs

A supply chain is the whole sequence of organisations involved in getting a product to the customer. Poor supplier choice causes production delays, lost sales and reputational damage.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain the impact on a restaurant of using an unreliable supplier. (2)

Model answer: An unreliable supplier may deliver late or substitute items, so the kitchen cannot produce menu dishes (1). This leads to disappointed customers, lost sales and damage to the restaurant's reputation (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Listing criteria without explaining the consequence of getting them wrong. Also, forgetting the ethical dimension — SQA rewards answers mentioning fair trade and sustainability.

Practice

1

Give two advantages of using a local supplier. (2)

Show answer

Lower transport costs and a faster, shorter delivery time; also better for the local community and the environment.

2

Explain the risk of relying on a single supplier. (2)

Show answer

If that supplier fails, strikes or increases prices, the business has no alternative source (1), so production may stop entirely and the business loses sales (2).

13Inventory management

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Inventory Management - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

Inventory (stock) is the materials and goods a business holds.

Three types:

  • Raw materials — not yet used
  • Work in progress — partly made
  • Finished goods — ready to sell

Costs of holding stock: storage, insurance, security, money tied up, risk of damage/obsolescence.

Costs of too little stock: lost sales, production stoppages, unhappy customers.

Methods: JIT (just-in-time) — order only when needed, reduces storage costs but risks delays. Buffer stock — minimum held to cover demand.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of holding buffer stock. (4)

Model answer: Buffer stock means the business can keep selling even if a delivery is late or demand suddenly increases (1), so it avoids lost sales and keeps customers happy (2). However, holding extra stock costs money in storage, insurance and security (1), and there is a risk it becomes obsolete or damaged before it is sold (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing JIT with 'no stock at all'. JIT means minimal stock, ordered to arrive just as needed. Also, only listing storage costs without mentioning the opposite risk of lost sales from too little stock.

Practice

1

Name the three types of inventory. (3)

Show answer

Raw materials; work in progress; finished goods.

2

Explain why a bakery might prefer JIT for flour. (2)

Show answer

Flour is bulky and can spoil or attract pests, so holding large amounts is costly and risky. JIT orders frequent smaller deliveries, reducing storage space and waste.

14Methods of production

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Int 2 Higher Business Management: Methods of Production

💡 The rule

  • Job production — one bespoke item at a time (a wedding dress, a custom kitchen). High quality, high cost, slow.
  • Batch production — a group of identical items made together, then the process changes (bakeries, furniture runs). Flexible but requires storage and set-up time.
  • Flow/mass production — continuous production of identical items on a line (cars, drinks). Cheap per unit but very inflexible and needs high investment.

Choose based on volume, customisation and cost.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why a furniture maker might use job production. (2)

Model answer: Each piece of furniture can be made to the customer's exact specifications in size, wood and finish (1). This gives high quality and customer satisfaction, which matters more than speed for bespoke products (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Saying flow production is 'always best because it is cheapest' — the answer must weigh the lack of flexibility. Also confusing batch and flow: batch changes to a different product; flow does not.

Practice

1

Which production method suits a company making 2 million identical bottles a day? (1)

Show answer

Flow/mass production.

2

Explain one disadvantage of job production. (2)

Show answer

Each item is made individually, so labour costs per unit are high and production is slow (1), meaning the business cannot easily meet a large sudden increase in demand (2).

15Quality management

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 and Higher Business Management: Quality Measures

💡 The rule

Quality control — checking finished products and rejecting faulty ones. Catches errors at the end, which wastes materials.

Quality assurance — building quality into every stage of production; checking at each step. Prevents errors rather than catching them.

Benefits of good quality: customer loyalty, fewer returns, premium pricing, good reputation, legal compliance.

Costs of poor quality: recalls, compensation, lost customers, bad publicity.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain the difference between quality control and quality assurance. (2)

Model answer: Quality control checks finished products and rejects those with faults (1). Quality assurance builds checks into every stage of production so faults are prevented before the product is finished (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Using the terms interchangeably. Also, only listing benefits without saying how the business achieves quality (training, standards, inspection).

Practice

1

Give two costs to a business of poor quality. (2)

Show answer

Products must be recalled or replaced, which is expensive; customers lose trust and go to competitors, reducing future sales.

2

Explain how staff training improves quality. (2)

Show answer

Trained staff make fewer mistakes because they understand the standards and correct procedures (1), so less time and material is wasted on faulty work (2).

16Human resource planning

⚖️ Either paper

▶ 2.1 INTRODUCTION TO HR MANAGEMENT / IB BUSINESS MANAGEMENT / human resources, HR planning, change

💡 The rule

Human resource planning ensures the business has the right number of staff, with the right skills, at the right time.

Steps: forecast demand → audit current staff → identify gaps → recruit/train/redeploy → review.

Workforce planning issues: staff turnover, absence, ageing workforce, skills shortages, seasonal demand.

Flexible working: part-time, job share, zero-hours, temporary contracts — reduces fixed costs but can reduce loyalty.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why a hotel might use part-time staff. (2)

Model answer: Demand varies, with busy weekends and quiet weekdays (1), so part-time staff let the hotel match staffing to demand and avoid paying for idle hours (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing HR planning with recruitment — planning comes first, and decides whether recruitment is even needed. Also, assuming part-time staff are always cheaper; training costs per head may rise.

Practice

1

State two consequences of high staff turnover. (2)

Show answer

Increased recruitment and training costs; loss of experienced staff and reduced service quality.

2

Explain one benefit of flexible working to an employee. (2)

Show answer

It allows them to fit work around family or study commitments (1), which improves work-life balance and can make them more motivated (2).

17Recruitment & selection

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5, Int 2 and Higher Business Management: RECRUITMENT

💡 The rule

Recruitment = attracting candidates. Selection = choosing the best one.

Internal recruitment — promote from within. Cheaper, faster, motivates staff; but brings no new ideas and leaves a gap.

External recruitment — advertise outside. Brings fresh skills and ideas; but costs more and takes longer.

Selection methods: application forms, CVs, interviews, testing, assessment centres, references.

Steps: job analysis → job description → person specification → advertise → shortlist → interview → select → contract.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of internal recruitment. (4)

Model answer: Internal recruitment is cheaper and quicker because the business already knows the candidate's abilities and does not need to advertise widely (1); it also motivates staff by showing promotion is possible (2). However, it brings no new ideas or experience into the business (1), and it leaves a vacancy in the employee's old job that must still be filled (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing the job description (what the job involves) with the person specification (the qualities the person needs). Also, listing recruitment methods without explaining their suitability.

Practice

1

Explain the difference between a job description and a person specification. (2)

Show answer

A job description lists the duties and responsibilities of the post. A person specification lists the qualifications, skills and qualities the ideal candidate should have.

2

Give two advantages of using interviews to select staff. (2)

Show answer

They let the employer meet candidates and assess personality and communication; they allow questions to be explored in depth.

18Training

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Training (Part 1) - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

Induction training — introduces new staff to the business, its policies, safety and colleagues. Reduces early mistakes.

On-the-job training — learning while doing the job, often from a mentor. Realistic and cheap, but can pass on bad habits.

Off-the-job training — at a college or training centre. Expert teaching and no production disruption, but expensive and the skills may not transfer directly.

Benefits: better quality, fewer accidents, higher motivation, legal compliance. Costs: money, lost production time, staff may leave after training.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why a construction firm uses on-the-job training. (2)

Model answer: Trainees learn practical skills on real sites with actual tools and materials (1), so they develop the exact techniques the firm needs while still contributing to work (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Saying training is 'good' without naming the type and its specific benefit/cost. Also forgetting that off-the-job training removes staff from production, which is a cost.

Practice

1

State two benefits of induction training. (2)

Show answer

New staff learn health and safety rules, reducing accidents; they understand policies and settle in faster, reducing early mistakes.

2

Explain one disadvantage of off-the-job training. (2)

Show answer

It is expensive to pay for courses and the employee's wages while they are absent (1), and the business loses their productive output during that time (2).

19Motivation

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Motivation - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

Financial motivators: wages/salaries, bonus, commission, piece rate, profit sharing, overtime, fringe benefits (company car, discounts).

Non-financial motivators: job rotation, job enrichment, autonomy, praise, promotion prospects, good working conditions, team working.

Theories to name:

  • Maslow — needs from basic (pay, safety) to higher (belonging, esteem, self-actualisation)
  • Herzberg — hygiene factors (pay, conditions) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, responsibility) create satisfaction

A motivated workforce: higher productivity, lower turnover, better quality.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how a bonus scheme could motivate staff. (2)

Model answer: A bonus rewards staff financially for hitting a target (1), which gives them a direct reason to work harder and increase output or quality (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Describing a motivator without explaining why it motivates. Also, assuming money motivates everyone equally — non-financial motivators matter for many staff, and Herzberg argues pay only prevents dissatisfaction.

Practice

1

Explain the difference between job rotation and job enrichment. (2)

Show answer

Job rotation moves staff between different tasks to add variety. Job enrichment gives staff more responsibility and autonomy within their role.

2

Give two non-financial motivators. (2)

Show answer

Praise and recognition; opportunities for promotion or greater responsibility.

20Sources of finance

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Sources of Finance - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

Internal sources (from inside the business):

  • Retained profit, sale of assets, owner's capital, working capital

External sources (from outside):

  • Short term: bank overdraft, trade credit
  • Medium term: bank loan, leasing, hire purchase
  • Long term: mortgage, share issue, debenture, grant, crowdfunding

Choose by amount needed, time period, cost, and whether the business wants to give away ownership.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain one advantage and one disadvantage of a bank loan. (4)

Model answer: A bank loan provides a large sum immediately (1) and can be repaid in fixed instalments, making budgeting easier (2). However, the business must pay interest, which increases the total cost (1), and the bank may refuse if the business has a poor credit record or require security (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing overdraft (short-term, flexible, expensive) with a loan (fixed sum, longer term). Also, recommending share issue for a small business — it gives away ownership and control, which most owners resist.

Practice

1

Which source suits a business needing cash for two weeks until customers pay? (1)

Show answer

A bank overdraft — short term and flexible.

2

Explain one advantage of retained profit as a source of finance. (2)

Show answer

It costs nothing in interest or fees and does not require the owner to give up any control or ownership (1), so all future profit stays with the business (2).

21Break-even

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Unit 5.5: Break-Even Analysis - IB Business Management

💡 The rule

Break-even is the level of sales where total revenue equals total costs — no profit, no loss.

Break-even quantity $=\dfrac{\text{fixed costs}}{\text{selling price} - \text{variable cost per unit}}$

  • Fixed costs do not change with output (rent, insurance)
  • Variable costs change with output (materials, packaging)
  • Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit

Margin of safety = actual sales − break-even sales.

📝 Worked example

Fixed costs £6,000. Selling price £20. Variable cost per unit £8.

Contribution per unit $=20-8=\pounds12$

Break-even $=\dfrac{6000}{12}=500$ units

If the business sells 650 units: margin of safety $=650-500=150$ units.

🚨 Exam trap

Subtracting variable cost from fixed cost instead of from the selling price. Also, forgetting that break-even is measured in units, not money. A lower break-even point is generally better — it means less risk.

Practice

1

Fixed costs £4,500; price £15; variable cost £6. Calculate break-even.

Show answer

Contribution $=15-6=\pounds9$. Break-even $=\dfrac{4500}{9}=500$ units.

2

Using the same figures, calculate the margin of safety at sales of 620 units.

Show answer

$620-500=120$ units

22Cash flow forecasting

⚖️ Either paper

▶ The Critical Role of Cash and Cash-Flow Forecasting in Business | ThinkIGCSE.com

💡 The rule

A cash flow forecast predicts money coming in and going out over a period.

Key terms:

  • Inflows — money received (sales, loans, investment)
  • Outflows — money paid (wages, materials, rent)
  • Net cash flow = inflows − outflows
  • Opening balance — cash at the start of the month
  • Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow

The closing balance becomes the next month's opening balance.

📝 Worked example

Opening balance £2,000. Inflows £5,000. Outflows £6,500.

Net cash flow $=5{,}000-6{,}500=-\pounds1{,}500$

Closing balance $=2{,}000+(-1{,}500)=\pounds500$

🚨 Exam trap

Forgetting to carry the closing balance forward as the next opening balance — this causes the whole forecast to be wrong. Also, a negative closing balance is not automatically failure, but it does mean the business needs additional finance.

Practice

1

Opening balance £800; inflows £2,400; outflows £3,600. Find the closing balance.

Show answer

Net $=2400-3600=-\pounds1{,}200$. Closing $=800-1200=-\pounds400$ (an overdraft position).

2

Why might a profitable business still run out of cash?

Show answer

Profit is recorded when a sale is made, but cash arrives later if customers buy on credit. If outflows such as wages and rent fall due before the cash is received, the business can run out of cash despite being profitable.

23Income statement

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Income Statements - N5 Business Management

💡 The rule

The income statement (trading, profit and loss account) calculates profit over a period.

$$\text{Gross profit} = \text{Sales revenue} - \text{Cost of goods sold}$$

$$\text{Net profit} = \text{Gross profit} - \text{Expenses}$$

  • Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − closing stock
  • Expenses are overheads: rent, wages, insurance, advertising

Gross profit margin $=\dfrac{\text{gross profit}}{\text{sales}}\times100$

📝 Worked example

Sales £80,000. Opening stock £5,000, purchases £40,000, closing stock £6,000. Expenses £18,000.

COGS $=5{,}000+40{,}000-6{,}000=\pounds39{,}000$

Gross profit $=80{,}000-39{,}000=\pounds41{,}000$

Net profit $=41{,}000-18{,}000=\pounds23{,}000$

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing gross and net profit. Gross is after cost of goods sold only; net is after all expenses. Also, mixing up purchases with cost of goods sold — you must adjust for stock.

Practice

1

Sales £50,000; COGS £30,000; expenses £12,000. Calculate gross and net profit.

Show answer

Gross $=50{,}000-30{,}000=\pounds20{,}000$. Net $=20{,}000-12{,}000=\pounds8{,}000$.

2

Calculate the gross profit margin for the figures above.

Show answer

$\dfrac{20{,}000}{50{,}000}\times100=40\%$

24Ratio analysis

⚖️ Either paper

▶ IB Business Management: 3 5 Profitability_Liquidity Ratios_Efficiency Ratios

💡 The rule

Ratios assess a business's performance and financial position.

Profitability:

  • Gross profit margin $=\dfrac{\text{gross profit}}{\text{sales}}\times100\%$
  • Net profit margin $=\dfrac{\text{net profit}}{\text{sales}}\times100\%$

Liquidity:

  • Current ratio $=\dfrac{\text{current assets}}{\text{current liabilities}}$ (ideal ≈ 2:1)
  • Acid test $=\dfrac{\text{current assets} - \text{stock}}{\text{current liabilities}}$ (ideal ≈ 1:1)

Efficiency:

  • Rate of stock turnover $=\dfrac{\text{cost of goods sold}}{\text{average stock}}$

Always compare — to last year or a rival — and comment on whether the result is good or concerning.

📝 Worked example

Current assets £60,000 (including stock £20,000). Current liabilities £25,000.

Current ratio $=\dfrac{60{,}000}{25{,}000}=2.4:1$ — healthy liquidity.

Acid test $=\dfrac{60{,}000-20{,}000}{25{,}000}=1.6:1$ — still comfortable.

The business can comfortably pay its short-term debts.

🚨 Exam trap

Calculating a ratio but not commenting on it — the analysis marks come from interpretation. Also, using the acid test without removing stock from current assets. Higher is not always better: too high a current ratio can mean cash is sitting idle.

Practice

1

A business has net profit £15,000 on sales of £120,000. Calculate the net profit margin.

Show answer

$\dfrac{15{,}000}{120{,}000}\times100=12.5\%$

2

Current assets £40,000, stock £15,000, current liabilities £30,000. Calculate the acid test ratio and comment.

Show answer

$\dfrac{40{,}000-15{,}000}{30{,}000}=\dfrac{25{,}000}{30{,}000}=0.83:1$. This is below the ideal 1:1, suggesting the business may struggle to pay short-term debts without selling stock.

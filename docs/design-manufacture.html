1The design process

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 - Design & Manufacture

💡 The rule

The design process is a structured sequence:

1. Identify the problem/need 2. Research — existing products, users, materials 3. Write the specification — criteria the product must meet 4. Generate ideas — sketches, brainstorming 5. Develop and model — refine, make prototypes 6. Evaluate — test against the specification 7. Manufacture

It is iterative — you return to earlier stages as problems emerge.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why evaluation happens before manufacture. (2)

Model answer: Evaluating the prototype against the specification identifies weaknesses while changes are still cheap and easy (1). Fixing a fault before production avoids the far greater cost of re-tooling or recalling finished products (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Describing the process as strictly linear. Real design loops back — testing often reveals the specification needs revising. Also, listing stages without explaining the purpose of each.

Practice

1

State the purpose of a product specification. (2)

Show answer

It lists the criteria the finished product must meet, giving designers a clear target and a basis for evaluating the final design.

2

Explain why modelling is important in the design process. (2)

Show answer

A model lets the designer test form, fit and function cheaply before committing to manufacture (1), revealing problems that are hard to see on a drawing (2).

2Research techniques

⚖️ Either paper

▶ FVWL RIC National 5 Design and Manufacture

💡 The rule

Primary research — you collect it: user interviews, questionnaires, observation, ergonomic testing, making and testing models.

Secondary research — already exists: market data, competitor analysis, standards (BS/ISO), material data sheets, online sources.

What to research: the user's needs, existing products, materials and their properties, manufacturing methods, costs, safety standards, sustainability.

Good research is specific to the design problem, not general.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how interviewing a user improves a design. (2)

Model answer: Interviewing the user reveals specific difficulties they have with existing products (1), so the designer can target the design at real problems rather than assumed ones (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Listing research methods without linking them to why they suit the particular design problem. Also, relying only on secondary research — it cannot tell you about the specific user's needs.

Practice

1

Give two examples of primary research for a new kitchen utensil. (2)

Show answer

Interviewing people who cook regularly about grip and comfort; observing users preparing food to see where existing utensils cause difficulty.

2

Explain one advantage of secondary research. (2)

Show answer

It is quick and cheap because the data already exists (1), so the designer can learn about standards and competitor products without spending time or money collecting new data (2).

3The product specification

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Nat 5 Common Workshop Machines

💡 The rule

The specification is a list of measurable criteria the product must satisfy, written after research and before ideas are generated.

It should cover:

  • Function — what it must do
  • Performance — how well (load, durability)
  • Ergonomics — fit to the user
  • Aesthetics — appearance
  • Materials — properties required
  • Cost — target price
  • Manufacture — production method and scale
  • Safety/standards — legal requirements
  • Sustainability — environmental impact

Criteria must be testable — 'lightweight' is weak; 'under 250 g' is strong.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why specification criteria should be measurable. (2)

Model answer: Measurable criteria can be objectively tested against the finished product (1), so the designer can prove whether the design meets the brief rather than relying on opinion (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Writing vague criteria such as 'must look nice' or 'must be strong'. These cannot be tested and score poorly. Also, writing the specification after generating ideas — it should guide the ideas.

Practice

1

Rewrite this weak criterion to make it measurable: 'The chair should be comfortable.'

Show answer

'The seat height should be 450 mm ± 10 mm and the backrest should support the lumbar region when seated for 60 minutes.'

2

Name four headings a specification should cover. (4)

Show answer

Function; performance; ergonomics; aesthetics (also acceptable: materials, cost, manufacture, safety, sustainability).

4Idea-generation techniques

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Higher Design and Manufacture Lesson 2: Idea Generation

💡 The rule

Brainstorming — generate many ideas quickly without judging them.

Morphological analysis — break the problem into parts and combine options systematically.

SCAMPER — Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse.

Analogy — borrow solutions from other fields (e.g. Velcro from burdock seeds).

Sketching and annotation — develop ideas visually, labelling key features.

Evaluation of ideas uses the specification as the scoring criteria.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how SCAMPER helps generate design ideas. (2)

Model answer: SCAMPER provides prompts that force the designer to look at a product from seven different angles (1), such as combining two functions or eliminating an unnecessary part, which produces ideas that free thinking alone would miss (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Treating brainstorming as the only technique. Also, generating ideas without annotating them — marks are awarded for notes explaining the thinking, not just the drawing.

Practice

1

What does the 'C' in SCAMPER stand for and give an example. (2)

Show answer

Combine. For example, combining a torch and a pen into one product so the user carries fewer items.

2

Why should ideas be evaluated against the specification? (2)

Show answer

The specification states what the product must achieve, so scoring ideas against it gives an objective basis for choosing (1) rather than selecting on personal preference (2).

5Modelling

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Manufacturing processes

💡 The rule

Models let designers test ideas before manufacture.

Types:

  • Card/paper models — quick form and scale checks
  • Foam board — ergonomic testing (grip, shape)
  • CAD (computer-aided design) — 3D modelling, rendering, stress testing, rapid changes
  • 3D printing / rapid prototyping — physical part from a CAD file
  • Virtual models — simulation without any physical material

Benefits: cheap, fast, reveals problems, communicates ideas to clients. Limits: models may not behave exactly like the final material.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain one advantage of CAD over a physical model. (2)

Model answer: CAD allows the designer to change dimensions and see the effect instantly without rebuilding anything (1), so many variations can be tested far more quickly and cheaply than by making each one by hand (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Saying CAD is 'better' without naming a specific advantage. Also forgetting the limits — a model in foam may not show how the real material performs under load.

Practice

1

Give two reasons a designer might make a foam model of a handle. (2)

Show answer

To test the grip and comfort in the hand before manufacture; to check the shape and size are ergonomically suitable cheaply and quickly.

2

State one limitation of a scale model. (1)

Show answer

It may not behave the same as the full-size product — for example, strength and weight do not always scale proportionally.

6Graphics in design

⚖️ Either paper

▶ N5 Design Factors 1 & 2 - Function and Performance

💡 The rule

Designers communicate through graphics.

  • Isometric drawings — 3D representation with 30 degree axes; shows three faces.
  • Orthographic projection — separate views (front, plan, end); first-angle or third-angle.
  • Sectional views — show internal detail, hatched.
  • Exploded views — parts separated to show assembly.
  • Working drawings — dimensions and tolerances needed for manufacture.
  • CAD drawings — accurate, editable, shareable.

Scale must be stated. Dimensions must be clear and complete — a manufacturer must be able to make the part from the drawing alone.

📝 Worked example

Q: Why is an exploded view useful in a technical drawing? (2)

Model answer: It shows how the parts fit together in the order they assemble (1), which helps both the manufacturer and the customer understand the assembly without taking the product apart (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing isometric with orthographic — isometric shows 3D, orthographic shows separate flat views. Also, omitting dimensions or units from a working drawing, which makes it unusable.

Practice

1

What is the difference between first-angle and third-angle projection? (2)

Show answer

In first-angle projection the view is placed on the opposite side of the object; in third-angle projection the view is placed on the same side as the object being viewed.

2

Why must a working drawing include tolerances? (2)

Show answer

Manufacturing cannot be perfectly exact, so tolerances state the acceptable variation (1) and allow the part to be made and still fit with others (2).

7Function & performance

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Materials revision

💡 The rule

Function is what the product does. Performance is how well it does it.

Considerations:

  • Does it do the job reliably?
  • Is it durable and safe?
  • Does it work in the intended environment?
  • How long will it last?
  • Is it easy to maintain and repair?

Designers test function by modelling and prototype testing, and evaluate performance against the specification.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why a product's intended environment affects material choice. (2)

Model answer: A product used outdoors must withstand rain, UV light and temperature change (1), so a material such as stainless steel or UV-stable plastic is chosen instead of mild steel that would rust (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Describing function without linking to material or design choices. Also, evaluating performance without reference to the specification criteria.

Practice

1

Give two functional requirements of a school bag. (2)

Show answer

It must carry the required weight without the straps breaking; it must protect the contents from rain.

2

Explain how testing a prototype improves performance. (2)

Show answer

Testing reveals where the design fails in real use (1), so the designer can strengthen or adjust those parts before manufacture, improving reliability (2).

8Market, aesthetics & ergonomics

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Ergonomics and Anthropometrics

💡 The rule

Market pull — the design responds to what customers want (demand-driven). Technology push — a new technology creates a product people did not know they wanted.

Aesthetics — how the product looks and feels: form, colour, texture, proportion, symmetry.

Ergonomics — how well the product fits the human body: grip size, reach, posture, force required, comfort in use. Anthropometric data (measurements of the human body) informs ergonomic design.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how anthropometric data helps design a computer mouse. (2)

Model answer: Anthropometric data gives the range of hand sizes in the population (1), so the designer can shape the mouse to fit most users comfortably and reduce strain during long use (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing aesthetics (appearance) with ergonomics (fit and comfort). Also, describing market pull and technology push without an example.

Practice

1

Give one example of technology push. (1)

Show answer

The smartphone — touchscreen and mobile data technology made it possible before consumers had asked for it.

2

State two ergonomic considerations for a kitchen knife handle. (2)

Show answer

Handle diameter should fit the average hand; the shape should prevent the hand slipping forward onto the blade.

9People who influence design

⚖️ Either paper

▶ SQA National 5 Design & Manufacture - The Design Factors ...

💡 The rule

Designers and movements that have shaped products:

  • Dieter Rams — 'less but better'; ten principles of good design (functional, honest, durable, environmentally friendly)
  • Charles and Ray Eames — moulded plywood and plastic furniture; ergonomics and mass production
  • Philippe Starck — playful, sculptural products
  • James Dyson — function-led innovation (cyclone vacuum)
  • Alessi — colourful, characterful domestic products
  • Bauhaus movement — form follows function; simple geometric design

Their influence shows in materials, form and manufacturing approach.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how Dieter Rams' principles influence modern product design. (2)

Model answer: Rams argued products should be functional, simple and long-lasting rather than decorative (1). This is visible in modern minimalist products that hide controls and use restrained colours, prioritising usability over ornament (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Naming a designer without saying what they did or how it influenced design. Also, describing aesthetics only — the marks are for design thinking and its impact on products.

Practice

1

State one of Dieter Rams' principles of good design. (1)

Show answer

'Good design is as little design as possible' (or: functional, durable, honest, environmentally friendly).

2

Explain the Bauhaus principle 'form follows function'. (2)

Show answer

It means the shape of a product should be determined by what it needs to do rather than decoration (1), so unnecessary ornament is removed and the design is efficient and easy to manufacture (2).

10Woods & manufactured boards

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Turning (wood and metal)

💡 The rule

Hardwoods (from deciduous trees): oak, mahogany, ash, beech — generally harder and more expensive.

Softwoods (from coniferous trees): pine, cedar, spruce — cheaper, easier to work, less durable.

Manufactured boards:

  • Plywood — layers at right angles, strong and stable
  • MDF — smooth, easy to machine, no grain, dust hazard
  • Chipboard — cheap, used for flat-pack furniture
  • Hardboard — thin, smooth, used for backs of units

Properties to know: strength, durability, weight, cost, appearance, workability, sustainability.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why plywood is stronger than solid wood of the same thickness. (2)

Model answer: Plywood is made from layers glued with the grain at right angles to each other (1). This cross-graining resists splitting and warping in any one direction, making it stronger and more stable than a single piece of solid timber (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Calling MDF or plywood a 'wood' — they are manufactured boards. Also, mixing up hardwood/softwood with hard/soft in terms of physical hardness; the terms refer to the tree type.

Practice

1

Give two properties of oak that make it suitable for furniture. (2)

Show answer

It is hard and durable, resisting wear; it has an attractive grain that finishes well.

2

Why is chipboard widely used in flat-pack furniture? (2)

Show answer

It is inexpensive to produce from waste wood (1) and can be veneered or laminated to look like solid wood, keeping costs low (2).

11Metals & plastics

⚖️ Either paper

▶ All of MATERIALS explained | GCSE & A-Level Design Technology

💡 The rule

Ferrous metals contain iron (mild steel, cast iron) — strong but rust unless protected. Non-ferrous contain no iron (aluminium, copper, brass) — resist rust. Alloys are mixtures (steel, brass, bronze).

Plastics:

  • Thermoplastics — soften when heated and can be reshaped/recycled (acrylic, polythene, PVC, nylon)
  • Thermosetting plastics — set permanently and cannot be remelted (urea formaldehyde, epoxy resin)

Choose by: strength, weight, corrosion resistance, cost, conductivity, workability.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why aluminium is used for aircraft bodies. (2)

Model answer: Aluminium is light for its strength (1), which reduces fuel consumption, and it forms a protective oxide layer that resists corrosion, so it lasts in wet conditions (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing thermoplastic and thermosetting — thermoplastics can be reheated and reshaped; thermosetting cannot. Also, saying aluminium does not corrode at all — it does form an oxide layer.

Practice

1

Give one difference between ferrous and non-ferrous metals. (2)

Show answer

Ferrous metals contain iron and are magnetic but rust unless protected; non-ferrous metals contain no iron and resist rust.

2

Explain why PVC is used for window frames. (2)

Show answer

PVC is a thermoplastic that does not corrode or rot and needs no painting (1), so it is low maintenance and durable outdoors (2).

12Measuring & marking-out tools

⚖️ Either paper

▶ 3 easy ways to make a Mortise and Tenon Joint

💡 The rule

Measuring: steel rule (mm), tape measure (long lengths), vernier caliper (precision to 0.1 mm), micrometer (precision to 0.01 mm).

Marking out:

  • Try square — 90 degree lines and checking squareness
  • Marking gauge — lines parallel to an edge
  • Mortise gauge — two parallel lines (mortise and tenon)
  • Scriber — marks metal (never a pencil on metal)
  • Centre punch — marks a point for drilling
  • Dividers — circles and arcs on metal
  • Mitre square — 45 degree angles

📝 Worked example

Q: Why is a centre punch used before drilling metal? (2)

Model answer: The centre punch creates a small indentation that stops the drill bit sliding off the marked point (1), so the hole is drilled in the correct position and the bit is less likely to break (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Using a pencil to mark metal — a scriber is required because pencil rubs off. Also, confusing the try square (90 degrees) with the mitre square (45 degrees).

Practice

1

Which tool measures to 0.01 mm? (1)

Show answer

A micrometer.

2

Explain the difference between a marking gauge and a mortise gauge. (2)

Show answer

A marking gauge has one pin and marks a single line parallel to an edge; a mortise gauge has two pins and marks two parallel lines at once for joints such as mortise and tenon.

13Hand tools

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Nat 5 Common Tools

💡 The rule

Cutting: tenon saw (straight, fine cuts in wood), coping saw (curved cuts), hacksaw (metal), chisel (paring and cutting joints).

Shaping: plane (flattening and smoothing wood), file (shaping and smoothing metal), rasp (rough shaping wood).

Drilling/holding: hand drill, brace, G-clamp, bench vice, sash clamp.

Striking: claw hammer (nails), mallet (chisels — protects the handle), ball-peen hammer (metalwork).

Safety: always clamp work, cut away from the body, use the correct saw for the material.

📝 Worked example

Q: Why is a mallet used with a wood chisel rather than a hammer? (2)

Model answer: A mallet has a soft head that drives the chisel without damaging its wooden handle (1), whereas a metal hammer would split or mushroom the handle and ruin the tool (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Naming a tool without knowing its purpose. Also, using a hacksaw on wood or a tenon saw on metal — examiners test tool-to-material matching.

Practice

1

Which saw would you use to cut a curve in plywood? (1)

Show answer

A coping saw.

2

Explain why a G-clamp is used when drilling. (2)

Show answer

It holds the workpiece firmly to the bench (1), so it cannot spin or move while drilling, which prevents injury and inaccurate holes (2).

14Machine tools

⚖️ Either paper

▶ What is DFMA®? Design for Manufacture and Assembly ...

💡 The rule

Pillar drill — fixed drill for accurate vertical holes; use a guard, clamp the work, select the right speed.

Scroll saw — fine curved cuts in thin material.

Band saw — straight and curved cuts in thicker material.

Lathe — turning cylindrical shapes (wood or metal).

Disc/belt sander — finishing surfaces.

CNC machines — computer-controlled, repeatable and accurate; expensive to set up but ideal for batch production.

Safety: guards in place, correct bit/blade, eye protection, no loose clothing.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain one advantage of a CNC machine over a manual machine tool. (2)

Model answer: A CNC machine follows a stored program, so every part is identical to within very tight tolerances (1). This consistency means less waste and no variation between operators, which matters in batch production (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Saying CNC is 'faster' without mentioning consistency and repeatability, which is the key benefit examiners look for. Also forgetting the high set-up cost and need for programming skills.

Practice

1

State two safety precautions when using a pillar drill. (2)

Show answer

Clamp the workpiece and wear eye protection; ensure the chuck guard is in place before starting.

2

Explain why a CNC lathe suits batch production. (2)

Show answer

Once programmed, it repeats identical operations without variation (1), so hundreds of identical parts can be made accurately with less skilled labour per unit (2).

15Joining & assembly

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Technology Interventions in DfMA Workflows and Precision Engineering

💡 The rule

Permanent joints:

  • Nails and screws (screws allow dismantling), glue (PVA for wood), welding/brazing (metal), rivets
  • Wood joints: butt, lap, mortise and tenon, dovetail, dowel — each trades strength against difficulty

Temporary/knock-down fittings: bolts, nuts, cam locks, hinges (allow flat-pack assembly)

Choose by: strength needed, whether it must come apart, materials, appearance, cost.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why knock-down fittings are used in flat-pack furniture. (2)

Model answer: They allow the product to be shipped flat and assembled by the customer (1), which reduces transport and storage costs dramatically compared with shipping assembled furniture (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing permanent and temporary joints. Also, recommending a joint without considering the material — welding only works on metals, not wood.

Practice

1

Which wood joint is strongest for a drawer corner and why? (2)

Show answer

A dovetail joint — the interlocking angled pins resist being pulled apart, making it very strong for drawer corners.

2

Explain when screws are preferable to nails. (2)

Show answer

Screws hold more securely and can be removed later for dismantling or repair (1), whereas nails are driven in permanently and can split thin wood (2).

16Finishing

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Surface finishes

💡 The rule

Finishes protect the material and improve appearance.

Wood finishes: varnish (hard-wearing, waterproof), wood stain (colour), wax (subtle sheen), paint (colour, hides grain), oil (penetrates and protects).

Metal finishes: paint, galvanising (zinc coating, rust protection), electroplating (chrome, nickel), anodising (aluminium), powder coating (durable colour).

Plastic finishes: usually none needed; may be polished or textured during manufacture.

Finishing is often the final stage before assembly.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why garden furniture is galvanised rather than painted. (2)

Model answer: Galvanising coats the steel in zinc, which corrodes sacrificially to protect the metal underneath (1). This lasts far longer outdoors than paint, which chips and lets rust start at the exposed edge (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Choosing a finish without saying what it protects against. Also, forgetting that a finish must suit the material — varnish on metal does nothing useful.

Practice

1

Give two reasons for applying a finish to a wooden table. (2)

Show answer

To protect the wood from moisture and staining; to improve its appearance and enhance the grain.

2

Explain the purpose of anodising aluminium. (2)

Show answer

It thickens the natural oxide layer on the surface (1), improving corrosion resistance and allowing coloured dyes to be absorbed for a decorative finish (2).

17Commercial manufacture

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Ready...Steady...FAB...Design & Manufacture / N5

💡 The rule

Scale of production changes the method:

  • One-off — bespoke, high skill, high cost per unit
  • Batch — set number produced, then changeover; needs jigs and storage
  • Mass/continuous — high volume, automated, low cost per unit

Enablers of commercial production: jigs and fixtures (speed and consistency), CNC, standardised parts, division of labour, automation.

Costs: set-up costs are high but unit costs fall as volume rises (economies of scale).

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how a jig improves commercial manufacture. (2)

Model answer: A jig holds the workpiece in the same position every time (1), so repeated operations such as drilling are faster and every part comes out identical, reducing waste and rework (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Confusing a jig (holds and guides the tool) with a fixture (holds the work only). Also, saying mass production is always better — it needs huge investment and offers no flexibility.

Practice

1

Explain the difference between a jig and a fixture. (2)

Show answer

A jig holds the workpiece and guides the tool to the correct position. A fixture only holds the workpiece securely without guiding the tool.

2

Why do unit costs fall as production volume increases? (2)

Show answer

Set-up costs and tooling are spread over many more units (1), and automated production reduces labour cost per item (2).

18Impact & sustainability

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Episode 5 - Manufacturing a Greener Future

💡 The rule

Design decisions have environmental and social impacts.

The 6 Rs: Rethink, Refuse, Reduce, Reuse, Repair, Recycle.

Consider:

  • Material source — renewable? recycled? sustainably certified (FSC timber)?
  • Energy use in manufacture and in use
  • Transport — locally sourced reduces carbon
  • Lifetime — durable and repairable beats disposable
  • End of life — can it be disassembled and recycled?
  • Social impact — fair labour, safe working conditions

Life cycle assessment (LCA) evaluates impact from raw material to disposal.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain how designing for disassembly reduces environmental impact. (2)

Model answer: If a product can be taken apart easily, components and materials can be separated and recycled or replaced (1), rather than the whole product being sent to landfill when one part fails (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Only mentioning recycling. The strongest answers discuss material choice, energy, transport, lifetime and end of life. Also, assuming 'natural' materials are automatically more sustainable — they may be transported long distances.

Practice

1

Give two ways a designer can reduce a product's environmental impact. (2)

Show answer

Use recycled or sustainably sourced materials; design the product to be durable and repairable so it lasts longer.

2

Explain why locally sourcing materials can be more sustainable. (2)

Show answer

Shorter transport distances reduce fuel use and carbon emissions (1), and local sourcing can support the regional economy (2).

19Planning for manufacture & evaluation

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 - Computing Science

💡 The rule

Planning for manufacture includes:

  • A production plan / flow chart of operations in order
  • Materials list (with quantities and costs)
  • Tools and equipment required
  • Health and safety risk assessment
  • Quality checks at key stages
  • Time plan

Evaluation after manufacture tests the product against the specification, identifies strengths and weaknesses, and suggests improvements.

📝 Worked example

Q: Explain why a risk assessment is needed before manufacture. (2)

Model answer: It identifies hazards such as sharp tools, dust or electrical equipment and states the controls needed to reduce them (1). This protects workers from injury and ensures the business meets health and safety law (2).

🚨 Exam trap

Evaluating only the appearance. A proper evaluation covers function, performance, ergonomics, safety, cost and sustainability, and must refer back to the specification criteria.

Practice

1

List four items that should appear in a production plan. (4)

Show answer

Sequence of operations; materials and quantities; tools and equipment; quality checks (also acceptable: time plan, health and safety notes).

2

Explain why evaluation should refer to the specification. (2)

Show answer

The specification states the criteria the product was designed to meet (1), so measuring against it gives an objective judgement of success rather than personal opinion (2).

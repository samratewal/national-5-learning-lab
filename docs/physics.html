1Vectors & scalars

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Dynamics | Vector & Scalar Quantities | THEORY

💡 The rule

  • Scalar — magnitude only, no direction. Examples: distance, speed, mass, time, energy, temperature.
  • Vector — magnitude and direction. Examples: displacement, velocity, force, acceleration, weight.
  • Resultant of two perpendicular vectors ($a$ and $b$): magnitude $R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$; direction $\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$ measured from $a$.
  • One dimension: add vectors in the same direction; subtract vectors acting in opposite directions.
  • Units are those of the vectors themselves, e.g. m or m s$^{-1}$.

📝 Worked example

A walker goes 4.0 m east then 3.0 m north.

$R=\sqrt{4.0^2+3.0^2}=\sqrt{16+9}=\sqrt{25}=5.0\ \text{m}$

$\theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{3.0}{4.0}\right)=36.9^\circ$ north of east.

Both data values have 2 significant figures, so the resultant is 5.0 m at 37° north of east.

🚨 Exam trap

  • Distance vs displacement. Distance is a scalar (total path length); displacement is a vector (straight line from start to finish, with direction). An answer of only "5 m" loses the direction mark.
  • Adding magnitudes blindly. $4.0+3.0=7.0$ m is wrong — the vectors are at right angles, so use Pythagoras.
  • Omitting the direction (e.g. "north of east") even when the magnitude is correct.
  • Using Pythagoras when the vectors are not at right angles — then a scale diagram is needed.

Practice

1

A model boat is pulled by two ropes at right angles: 6.0 N east and 8.0 N north. Find the resultant force.

Show answer

$R=\sqrt{6.0^2+8.0^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10\ ext{N}$ (10 N, 2 s.f.).

$ heta= an^{-1}\!\left( rac{8.0}{6.0} ight)=53.1^\circ$ north of east.

Resultant = 10 N at 53° north of east.

2

A car travels 30 m east, then 40 m west. State the distance and the displacement.

Show answer

Distance (scalar) $=30+40=70\ ext{m}$.

Displacement (vector) $=30-40=-10\ ext{m}$, i.e. magnitude 10 m in the west direction.

Distance = 70 m; displacement = 10 m west.

2Velocity–time graphs

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Drawing Velocity-Time Graphs - National 5 Physics

💡 The rule

On a velocity–time graph:

  • Gradient $=\dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}=$ acceleration (m s$^{-2}$).
  • Area under the graph $=$ displacement (m).
  • A horizontal line means constant velocity (zero acceleration); a straight sloping line means uniform acceleration; a negative gradient means deceleration.
  • Split a complex graph into rectangles and triangles to find the area. Areas below the time axis count as negative displacement.
  • Units: velocity in m s$^{-1}$, time in s, area in m.

📝 Worked example

A cyclist accelerates from 0 to 20 m/s in 5.0 s, travels at 20 m/s for 10 s, then brakes to rest in 4.0 s.

Acceleration (first 5.0 s): $a=\dfrac{v-u}{t}=\dfrac{20-0}{5.0}=4.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}$

Displacement = area under the graph:

  • first triangle: $\tfrac12\times5.0\times20=50$ m
  • rectangle: $10\times20=200$ m
  • second triangle: $\tfrac12\times4.0\times20=40$ m

$s=50+200+40=290\ \text{m}$ (2 s.f.).

Acceleration = 4.0 m s$^{-2}$; displacement = 290 m.

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using the gradient to find distance. The gradient gives acceleration; the area gives displacement.
  • Using the wrong base. A triangle's area is $\tfrac12\times$ base (time) $\times$ height (velocity), not $\tfrac12\times v\times v$.
  • Ignoring negative areas. Motion below the time axis reduces displacement; it does not add to it.
  • Mixing units. Convert km h$^{-1}$ to m s$^{-1}$ ($\div3.6$) and minutes to seconds before reading the graph.

Practice

1

A van's velocity rises from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 4.0 s. Find its acceleration.

Show answer

$a=\dfrac{v-u}{t}=\dfrac{30-10}{4.0}=\dfrac{20}{4.0}=5.0\ ext{m s}^{-2}$.

Acceleration = 5.0 m s$^{-2}$ (2 s.f.).

2

A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 12 m/s in 8.0 s. Use the graph area to find the distance travelled.

Show answer

The graph is a triangle with base 8.0 s and height 12 m/s.

$s= frac12 imes8.0 imes12=48\ ext{m}$.

Distance = 48 m (2 s.f.).

3Acceleration

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Acceleration & Deceleration | WORKED EXAMPLES

💡 The rule

  • Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: $a=\dfrac{v-u}{t}$.
  • $u$ = initial velocity, $v$ = final velocity, $t$ = time (m s$^{-1}$, s).
  • SI unit: m s$^{-2}$ (metres per second squared).
  • Acceleration is a vector. Negative acceleration means slowing down (when motion is positive) or acceleration in the opposite direction.

📝 Worked example

A car speeds up from 12 m s$^{-1}$ to 27 m s$^{-1}$ in 5.0 s.

$a=\dfrac{v-u}{t}=\dfrac{27-12}{5.0}=\dfrac{15}{5.0}=3.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}$

Acceleration = 3.0 m s$^{-2}$ (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Subtracting the wrong way round. Use $v-u$ (final minus initial), not $u-v$.
  • Forgetting to convert units. 72 km h$^{-1}$ becomes $72\div3.6=20$ m s$^{-1}$; minutes must become seconds.
  • Losing the negative sign for deceleration. A train slowing from 25 m/s to rest in 20 s has $a=-1.25$ m s$^{-2}$.
  • Rounding too early. Keep the full value until the final answer, then round to the least number of significant figures in the data.

Practice

1

A cyclist accelerates from rest to 8.0 m/s in 4.0 s. Find the acceleration.

Show answer

$a=\dfrac{8.0-0}{4.0}=2.0\ ext{m s}^{-2}$.

Acceleration = 2.0 m s$^{-2}$ (2 s.f.).

2

A train travelling at 25 m/s decelerates uniformly to rest in 20 s. Find its acceleration.

Show answer

$a=\dfrac{0-25}{20}=-1.25\ ext{m s}^{-2}$.

Acceleration = $-1.25$ m s$^{-2}$ (a deceleration of 1.25 m s$^{-2}$; to 2 s.f. this is $-1.3$ m s$^{-2}$).

4Newton's laws

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Dynamics | Newton's First Law | THEORY

💡 The rule

  • Newton's first law: balanced forces → an object stays at rest or moves with constant velocity.
  • Newton's second law: an unbalanced force causes acceleration: $F_{\text{unbalanced}}=ma$.
  • Newton's third law: forces occur in equal and opposite pairs acting on different objects.
  • Weight: $W=mg$, where $g$ is the gravitational field strength (N kg$^{-1}$).
  • Units: $F$ and $W$ in N, $m$ in kg, $a$ in m s$^{-2}$, $g$ in N kg$^{-1}$.

📝 Worked example

A 1200 kg car experiences a driving force of 2800 N and a drag force of 400 N.

Unbalanced force: $F=2800-400=2400\ \text{N}$

$a=\dfrac{F}{m}=\dfrac{2400}{1200}=2.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}$

Weight on Earth: $W=mg=1200\times9.8=11760\ \text{N}\approx1.2\times10^4\ \text{N}$.

Acceleration = 2.0 m s$^{-2}$; weight = $1.2\times10^4$ N.

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using the total force instead of the unbalanced force. Subtract opposing forces first.
  • Confusing mass and weight. Mass (kg) is constant; weight (N) depends on $g$.
  • Using $g=10$ when the data sheet gives 9.8 (unless the question states otherwise).
  • Thinking action–reaction forces cancel. They act on different objects, so they do not cancel on the same object.
  • Forgetting direction/sign when forces act in opposite directions.

Practice

1

Find the weight of an 80 kg astronaut on the Moon ($g=1.6$ N kg$^{-1}$).

Show answer

$W=mg=80 imes1.6=128\ ext{N}$.

Weight = 128 N ($approx1.3 imes10^2$ N, 2 s.f.).

2

A 5.0 kg box has an unbalanced force of 20 N acting on it. Find its acceleration.

Show answer

$a=\dfrac{F}{m}=\dfrac{20}{5.0}=4.0\ ext{m s}^{-2}$.

Acceleration = 4.0 m s$^{-2}$ (2 s.f.).

5Energy

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Dynamics | Conservation of Energy | THEORY

💡 The rule

  • Work done: $E_w=Fd$ (J), where $F$ is the unbalanced force (N) and $d$ is the distance (m).
  • Gravitational potential energy: $E_p=mgh$ (J).
  • Kinetic energy: $E_k=\tfrac12 mv^2$ (J).
  • Conservation of energy: energy is transferred, not created or destroyed. In a closed system with no friction, $E_k=E_p$.
  • Units: J = N m = kg m$^2$ s$^{-2}$.

📝 Worked example

A 2.0 kg ball is dropped from 5.0 m. Find its speed just before impact (ignore air resistance).

$E_p=mgh=2.0\times9.8\times5.0=98\ \text{J}$

All of this becomes kinetic energy: $\tfrac12 mv^2=98$

$v=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\times98}{2.0}}=\sqrt{98}=9.9\ \text{m s}^{-1}$

Speed = 9.9 m s$^{-1}$ (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Forgetting energy transfers. Some energy may become heat or sound, so the final $E_k$ is often less than $E_p$.
  • Mixing up $E_p$ and $E_k$, or using the wrong height. Use the vertical height in metres.
  • Not converting cm to m or g to kg before calculating.
  • Rounding too early, especially inside the square root.
  • Using $g=10$ when the data sheet supplies 9.8.

Practice

1

A 60 kg student climbs a 4.0 m staircase. Find the gain in gravitational potential energy.

Show answer

$E_p=mgh=60 imes9.8 imes4.0=2352\ ext{J}$.

Gain in $E_p$ = 2352 J ($approx2.4 imes10^3$ J, 2 s.f.).

2

Find the kinetic energy of a 1500 kg car travelling at 20 m/s.

Show answer

$E_k= frac12 mv^2= frac12 imes1500 imes20^2=0.5 imes1500 imes400=3.0 imes10^5\ ext{J}$.

Kinetic energy = $3.0 imes10^5$ J (2 s.f.).

6Projectile motion

⚖️ Either paper

▶ Projectiles (National 5 Physics)

💡 The rule

  • Projectile motion is split into horizontal and vertical motion.
  • Horizontal: constant velocity (no horizontal acceleration), so $s_h=v_h t$.
  • Vertical: constant acceleration $g$ downwards. For a horizontal launch $u_v=0$, so $v_v=u_v+at$; height can be found from the area under a vertical $v$–$t$ graph, or from $s=\tfrac{u+v}{2}t$.
  • The time is the same for both directions.
  • Units: distance m, velocity m s$^{-1}$, time s, acceleration m s$^{-2}$.

📝 Worked example

A ball is launched horizontally at 8.0 m s$^{-1}$ from a height of 45 m. Find the horizontal range.

Vertical: $s=45$ m, $u_v=0$, $a=9.8$ m s$^{-2}$. Using $s=\tfrac12 at^2$:

$t=\sqrt{\dfrac{2s}{g}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{2\times45}{9.8}}=\sqrt{9.18}=3.03\ \text{s}$

Horizontal: $s_h=v_h t=8.0\times3.03=24.2\ \text{m}$

Range = 24 m (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using the horizontal velocity in vertical calculations (or vice versa). Keep the components separate.
  • Forgetting $u_v=0$ for a horizontal launch.
  • Using $g$ as 10 when the data sheet supplies 9.8.
  • Mixing up range (horizontal) and height (vertical).
  • Thinking a dropped object lands at a different time from a horizontally fired one — their vertical motions are identical.

Practice

1

A ball is fired horizontally at 12 m/s from a height of 20 m. Find the time of flight and the horizontal range ($g=9.8$ m s$^{-2}$).

Show answer

Vertical: $t=\sqrt{\dfrac{2 imes20}{9.8}}=\sqrt{4.08}=2.02\ ext{s}$.

Horizontal: $s_h=v_h t=12 imes2.02=24.2\ ext{m}$.

Time = 2.0 s; range = 24 m (2 s.f.).

2

Explain why a ball dropped from a height and an identical ball fired horizontally from the same height hit the ground at the same time.

Show answer

Both have the same vertical initial velocity ($u_v=0$) and the same vertical acceleration ($g=9.8$ m s$^{-2}$). Horizontal velocity does not affect vertical motion, so both take the same time to fall the same height.

Same time of flight because the vertical motions are identical.

7Space exploration

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Space | Risks of Space Exploration

💡 The rule

  • Weight in a gravitational field: $W=mg$, where $g$ varies across the universe (Earth 9.8, Moon 1.6, Mars 3.7 N kg$^{-1}$).
  • Orbital speed for a circular orbit: $v=\dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ (circumference ÷ period).
  • Geostationary satellites have a period of 24 hours and orbit at an altitude of about 36 000 km.
  • A satellite in a higher orbit has a longer period than one in a lower orbit.
  • Units: N, N kg$^{-1}$, m, s, m s$^{-1}$.

📝 Worked example

A satellite orbits Earth in a circle of radius $4.2\times10^7$ m with a period of 24 hours. Find its orbital speed.

$T=24\ \text{h}=24\times3600=86\,400\ \text{s}$

$v=\dfrac{2\pi r}{T}=\dfrac{2\pi\times4.2\times10^7}{86\,400}=3.05\times10^3\ \text{m s}^{-1}$

Orbital speed = $3.1\times10^3$ m s$^{-1}$ (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using altitude instead of orbital radius. $r=R_{\text{Earth}}+\text{altitude}$.
  • Forgetting to convert hours to seconds ($\times3600$).
  • Using the surface value of $g$ for an orbiting satellite — $g$ decreases with distance.
  • Confusing mass and weight. An astronaut's mass is unchanged in space, but weight changes with $g$.
  • Mixing up period and orbital speed in the orbit relationship.

Practice

1

Find the weight of a 1500 kg satellite on the surface of Mars ($g=3.7$ N kg$^{-1}$).

Show answer

$W=mg=1500 imes3.7=5550\ ext{N}$.

Weight = 5550 N ($approx5.6 imes10^3$ N, 2 s.f.).

2

Explain why a satellite in a higher orbit takes longer to complete one orbit than a satellite in a lower orbit.

Show answer

A higher orbit has a larger orbital radius, so the satellite travels a longer circumference ($2\pi r$). The gravitational force is weaker at that distance, so its orbital speed is lower. A longer distance travelled at a lower speed means a longer period.

Larger radius → longer circumference and lower speed → longer period.

8Cosmology

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Space | What You Should Know

💡 The rule

  • A light-year is the distance light travels in one year: $1\ \text{ly}=c\,t=3.0\times10^8\times3.15\times10^7=9.46\times10^{15}\ \text{m}$.
  • The Big Bang theory: the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from a very hot, dense state and has been expanding ever since.
  • Evidence includes the redshift of light from distant galaxies (they are moving away) and the cosmic microwave background radiation.
  • The whole electromagnetic spectrum is used to study astronomical objects; line spectra identify the elements present in stars.
  • Units: distance in metres. A light-year is a distance, not a time.

📝 Worked example

Convert 4.2 light-years to metres.

$1\ \text{ly}=9.46\times10^{15}\ \text{m}$

$4.2\ \text{ly}=4.2\times9.46\times10^{15}=3.97\times10^{16}\ \text{m}$

Distance = $4.0\times10^{16}$ m (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Treating a light-year as a time. It is a distance.
  • Using the wrong number of seconds in a year. Use $3.15\times10^7$ s, not 365.
  • Rounding the speed of light or the year length too early. Keep $3.0\times10^8$ and $3.15\times10^7$ until the final step.
  • Forgetting the direction of redshift: a redshift means the source is moving away (receding).
  • Mixing up continuous and line spectra: line spectra identify elements; continuous spectra come from hot, dense objects.

Practice

1

Convert 10 light-years to metres (use $1\ ext{ly}=9.46 imes10^{15}$ m).

Show answer

$10 imes9.46 imes10^{15}=9.46 imes10^{16}\ ext{m}$.

Distance = $9.5 imes10^{16}$ m (2 s.f.).

2

A distant galaxy is $1.9 imes10^{17}$ m away. Express this distance in light-years.

Show answer

$1.9 imes10^{17}\div9.46 imes10^{15}=20.1$.

Distance = 20 light-years (2 s.f.).

9Charge carriers & current

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Electricity | What You Should Know

💡 The rule

  • Electric current is the electric charge transferred per unit time: $I=\dfrac{Q}{t}$, or $Q=It$.
  • $Q$ = charge (C), $I$ = current (A), $t$ = time (s). 1 C = 1 A s.
  • Direct current (d.c.) flows in one direction; alternating current (a.c.) changes direction periodically.
  • In a series circuit the current is the same at every point.
  • Units: charge in coulombs (C), current in amperes (A), time in seconds (s).

📝 Worked example

A current of 0.50 A flows for 3.0 minutes. Find the charge transferred.

$t=3.0\ \text{min}=3.0\times60=180\ \text{s}$

$Q=It=0.50\times180=90\ \text{C}$

Charge = 90 C (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds ($\times60$).
  • Confusing charge and current. Charge is measured in coulombs; current in amperes.
  • Not converting milliamps to amps. 1 mA $=1\times10^{-3}$ A.
  • Thinking current is "used up" in a circuit — the current leaving a series component equals the current entering it.

Practice

1

A current of 2.0 A flows for 5.0 minutes. Calculate the charge transferred.

Show answer

$t=5.0 imes60=300\ ext{s}$; $Q=It=2.0 imes300=600\ ext{C}$.

Charge = 600 C (2 s.f.).

2

A charge of 15 C passes a point in 30 s. Find the current.

Show answer

$I=\dfrac{Q}{t}=\dfrac{15}{30}=0.50\ ext{A}$.

Current = 0.50 A (2 s.f.).

10Potential difference

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Electricity | Voltage | THEORY

💡 The rule

  • Potential difference (voltage) is the energy transferred per unit charge: $V=\dfrac{E}{Q}$, or $E=QV$.
  • $V$ in volts (V), $E$ in joules (J), $Q$ in coulombs (C). 1 V = 1 J C$^{-1}$.
  • The potential difference of the supply is a measure of the energy given to the charge carriers in a circuit.
  • A charged particle in an electric field experiences a force; between oppositely charged parallel plates the field is uniform and the particle follows a parabolic path.

📝 Worked example

A 12 V supply transfers 60 J of energy to the charge passing through it. Find the charge.

$V=\dfrac{E}{Q}\ \Rightarrow\ Q=\dfrac{E}{V}=\dfrac{60}{12}=5.0\ \text{C}$

Charge = 5.0 C (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using $V=IR$ when energy and charge are given. Use $V=E/Q$ (or $E=QV$).
  • Confusing the two voltage relationships. $V=E/Q$ concerns energy per charge; $V=IR$ concerns Ohm's law.
  • Not converting kJ to J before substituting.
  • Thinking potential difference is "used up". Components transfer energy; the pd is the energy transferred per unit charge.

Practice

1

A 6.0 V supply transfers energy to 2.0 C of charge. Find the energy transferred.

Show answer

$E=QV=2.0 imes6.0=12\ ext{J}$.

Energy = 12 J (2 s.f.).

2

45 J of energy is transferred to 9.0 C of charge. Find the potential difference.

Show answer

$V=\dfrac{E}{Q}=\dfrac{45}{9.0}=5.0\ ext{V}$.

Potential difference = 5.0 V (2 s.f.).

11Ohms law

⚖️ Either paper

▶ N5 Physics - Ohm's Law demo and graph

💡 The rule

  • Ohm's law: the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided its temperature is constant: $V=IR$.
  • $R=\dfrac{V}{I}$; unit ohm ($\Omega$), 1 $\Omega$ = 1 V A$^{-1}$.
  • On a $V$–$I$ graph, resistance is the gradient of the line of best fit.
  • For most metals, increasing the temperature increases the resistance (e.g. a filament lamp).
  • Units: V, A, $\Omega$.

📝 Worked example

A potential difference of 6.0 V is across a resistor and the current is 0.25 A. Find its resistance.

$R=\dfrac{V}{I}=\dfrac{6.0}{0.25}=24\ \Omega$

Resistance = 24 $\Omega$ (2 s.f.).

Using the same relationship the other way: a current of 0.40 A through a 15 $\Omega$ resistor needs $V=IR=0.40\times15=6.0$ V.

🚨 Exam trap

  • Rearranging incorrectly. Use $R=V/I$ and $I=V/R$, not $R=I/V$.
  • Forgetting to convert mA to A. 250 mA $=0.250$ A.
  • Using the gradient of an $I$–$V$ graph. Resistance is the gradient of a $V$–$I$ graph.
  • Assuming resistance is constant for a lamp. A filament lamp heats up, so its resistance changes.

Practice

1

A potential difference of 12 V is across a resistor carrying 3.0 A. Find the resistance.

Show answer

$R=\dfrac{V}{I}=\dfrac{12}{3.0}=4.0\ \Omega$.

Resistance = 4.0 $\Omega$ (2 s.f.).

2

A current of 0.20 A flows through a 50 $\Omega$ resistor. Find the potential difference across it.

Show answer

$V=IR=0.20 imes50=10\ ext{V}$.

Potential difference = 10 V (2 s.f.).

12Series & parallel circuits

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Electricity | Parallel Circuits | THEORY

💡 The rule

  • Series circuit: $I_T=I_1=I_2=\dots$; $V_T=V_1+V_2+\dots$; $R_T=R_1+R_2+\dots$
  • Parallel circuit: $V_T=V_1=V_2=\dots$; $I_T=I_1+I_2+\dots$; $\dfrac{1}{R_T}=\dfrac{1}{R_1}+\dfrac{1}{R_2}+\dots$
  • Adding resistors in series increases the total resistance; adding them in parallel decreases it.
  • For two resistors in parallel, $R_T$ is always smaller than the smallest individual resistor.
  • Units: current A, potential difference V, resistance $\Omega$.

📝 Worked example

A 6.0 V supply is connected to a 4.0 $\Omega$ and a 12.0 $\Omega$ resistor.

In series: $R_T=4.0+12.0=16\ \Omega$, so $I=\dfrac{V}{R_T}=\dfrac{6.0}{16}=0.375\ \text{A}$.

In parallel: $\dfrac{1}{R_T}=\dfrac{1}{4.0}+\dfrac{1}{12.0}=\dfrac{3}{12}+\dfrac{1}{12}=\dfrac{4}{12}$, so $R_T=3.0\ \Omega$.

$I_T=\dfrac{V}{R_T}=\dfrac{6.0}{3.0}=2.0\ \text{A}$

Series: $R_T=16\ \Omega$, $I=0.38$ A. Parallel: $R_T=3.0\ \Omega$, $I_T=2.0$ A.

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using series rules for a parallel circuit. Current is not the same in parallel branches.
  • Forgetting to invert. $\dfrac{1}{R_T}=\dfrac{1}{R_1}+\dfrac{1}{R_2}$ gives $1/R_T$; you must then invert to find $R_T$.
  • Adding resistances directly in parallel ($R_T=R_1+R_2$ is wrong for parallel).
  • Not checking the answer. A parallel total resistance must be less than the smallest resistor in the branch.

Practice

1

Two resistors of 3 $\Omega$ and 7 $\Omega$ are connected in series. Find the total resistance.

Show answer

$R_T=R_1+R_2=3+7=10\ \Omega$.

Total resistance = 10 $\Omega$.

2

Two resistors of 6 $\Omega$ and 3 $\Omega$ are connected in parallel. Find the total resistance.

Show answer

$\dfrac{1}{R_T}=\dfrac{1}{6}+\dfrac{1}{3}=\dfrac{1}{6}+\dfrac{2}{6}=\dfrac{3}{6}=\dfrac12$, so $R_T=2\ \Omega$.

Total resistance = 2 $\Omega$ (less than the smallest resistor, 3 $\Omega$, as expected).

13Electrical power

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Electrical Power | WORKED EXAMPLES

💡 The rule

  • Electrical power is the energy transferred per unit time: $P=\dfrac{E}{t}$.
  • Equivalent forms: $P=IV$, $P=I^2R$ and $P=\dfrac{V^2}{R}$.
  • $P$ in watts (W), $E$ in joules (J), $t$ in seconds (s); 1 W = 1 J s$^{-1}$.
  • Fuse choice at 230 V: a 3 A fuse for appliances rated up to 720 W; a 13 A fuse for appliances rated over 720 W.

📝 Worked example

A 230 V appliance draws a current of 0.50 A. Find its power and the energy it transfers in 2.0 minutes.

$P=IV=0.50\times230=115\ \text{W}$

$t=2.0\ \text{min}=120\ \text{s}$

$E=Pt=115\times120=13\,800\ \text{J}\approx1.4\times10^4\ \text{J}$

Since 115 W is less than 720 W, a 3 A fuse is suitable.

Power = 115 W; energy = $1.4\times10^4$ J; 3 A fuse.

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using time in minutes instead of seconds.
  • Choosing the wrong fuse. Select 3 A for up to 720 W and 13 A above 720 W.
  • Confusing $P=IV$ with $P=IR$. Use the form that matches the quantities given.
  • Not converting kW to W (1 kW = 1000 W) or minutes to seconds.

Practice

1

A 230 V appliance draws a current of 4.0 A. Find its power and state a suitable fuse rating.

Show answer

$P=IV=4.0 imes230=920\ ext{W}$.

Since 920 W is greater than 720 W, a 13 A fuse is suitable.

Power = 920 W; 13 A fuse.

2

A 60 W lamp is switched on for 5.0 minutes. Find the energy transferred.

Show answer

$t=5.0 imes60=300\ ext{s}$; $E=Pt=60 imes300=18\,000\ ext{J}$.

Energy = $1.8 imes10^4$ J (2 s.f.).

14Specific heat capacity

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Specific Heat Capacity | Properties of Matter

💡 The rule

  • Specific heat capacity $c$ is the heat energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a material by 1 °C.
  • $E_h=cm\Delta T$, where $\Delta T$ is the temperature change.
  • Units: $c$ in J kg$^{-1}$ °C$^{-1}$, $E_h$ in J, $m$ in kg, $\Delta T$ in °C.
  • Temperature is a measure of the mean kinetic energy of the particles of a substance.
  • For a change in temperature, a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K, so either unit may be used for $\Delta T$.

📝 Worked example

2.0 kg of water is heated from 20 °C to 80 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4180 J kg$^{-1}$ °C$^{-1}$. Find the heat energy supplied.

$\Delta T=80-20=60\ ^\circ\text{C}$

$E_h=cm\Delta T=4180\times2.0\times60=501\,600\ \text{J}$

$E_h\approx5.0\times10^5\ \text{J}$

Heat energy = $5.0\times10^5$ J (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using the final temperature instead of $\Delta T$. Always subtract the initial temperature.
  • Not converting grams to kilograms (1 g $=1\times10^{-3}$ kg).
  • Using the wrong material's $c$ from the data sheet.
  • Ignoring heat lost to the surroundings, which makes the measured $c$ appear higher than the accepted value.

Practice

1

0.50 kg of aluminium (c = 902 J kg$^{-1}$ °C$^{-1}$) is heated through 40 °C. Find the heat energy supplied.

Show answer

$E_h=cm\Delta T=902 imes0.50 imes40=18\,040\ ext{J}$.

Heat energy = $1.8 imes10^4$ J (2 s.f.).

2

3.0 kg of water (c = 4180 J kg$^{-1}$ °C$^{-1}$) is heated through 25 °C. Find the heat energy supplied.

Show answer

$E_h=cm\Delta T=4180 imes3.0 imes25=313\,500\ ext{J}$.

Heat energy = $3.1 imes10^5$ J (2 s.f.).

15Specific latent heat

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Properties of Matter | Specific Latent Heat

💡 The rule

  • Specific latent heat $l$ is the heat energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a material without a change in temperature.
  • $E_h=ml$.
  • Fusion = solid ↔ liquid: water has $l=3.34\times10^5$ J kg$^{-1}$.
  • Vaporisation = liquid ↔ gas: water has $l=2.26\times10^6$ J kg$^{-1}$.
  • Units: $l$ in J kg$^{-1}$, $E_h$ in J, $m$ in kg.
  • During a change of state the temperature stays constant; the energy is used to break or form bonds between particles.

📝 Worked example

0.25 kg of ice at 0 °C is melted. The specific latent heat of fusion of water is $3.34\times10^5$ J kg$^{-1}$. Find the heat energy required.

$E_h=ml=0.25\times3.34\times10^5=83\,500\ \text{J}$

$E_h\approx8.4\times10^4\ \text{J}$

The temperature remains at 0 °C throughout the melting process.

Heat energy = $8.4\times10^4$ J (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using $E_h=cm\Delta T$ for a change of state. During melting or boiling $\Delta T=0$, so that relationship gives zero.
  • Using vaporisation when fusion is required (or vice versa). Match the change of state to the correct $l$.
  • Not converting grams to kilograms.
  • Forgetting the temperature is constant during the change of state.
  • Adding the energy for warming and melting incorrectly — they are separate stages.

Practice

1

0.10 kg of water at 100 °C is boiled into steam. The specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is $2.26 imes10^6$ J kg$^{-1}$. Find the heat energy required.

Show answer

$E_h=ml=0.10 imes2.26 imes10^6=2.26 imes10^5\ ext{J}$.

Heat energy = $2.3 imes10^5$ J (2 s.f.).

2

0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C is melted. The specific latent heat of fusion of water is $3.34 imes10^5$ J kg$^{-1}$. Find the heat energy required.

Show answer

$E_h=ml=0.50 imes3.34 imes10^5=1.67 imes10^5\ ext{J}$.

Heat energy = $1.7 imes10^5$ J (2 s.f.).

16Gas laws & the kinetic model

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Properties of Matter | The Kinetic Theory of Gases

💡 The rule

  • Pressure: $p=\dfrac{F}{A}$ (Pa = N m$^{-2}$).
  • Kelvin: $T(\text{K})=T(^\circ\text{C})+273$; absolute zero is $0\ \text{K}=-273\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
  • For a fixed mass of gas: $p_1V_1=p_2V_2$ (Boyle's law, constant $T$); $\dfrac{p_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{p_2}{T_2}$ (Gay-Lussac's law, constant $V$); $\dfrac{V_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$ (Charles's law, constant $p$); and $\dfrac{pV}{T}=\text{constant}$.
  • Kinetic model: gas pressure is caused by particles colliding with the container walls; temperature is related to the mean kinetic energy of the particles.

📝 Worked example

A fixed mass of gas at 100 kPa and 300 K is heated to 450 K at constant volume. Find the new pressure.

$\dfrac{p_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{p_2}{T_2}\ \Rightarrow\ p_2=\dfrac{p_1T_2}{T_1}$

$p_2=\dfrac{100\times450}{300}=150\ \text{kPa}$

New pressure = 150 kPa (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Forgetting to convert °C to K. Always add 273.
  • Inverting the ratio. Use $\dfrac{p_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{p_2}{T_2}$, not $\dfrac{p_1}{T_2}=\dfrac{p_2}{T_1}$.
  • Mixing volume units (e.g. cm$^3$ with m$^3$) without converting. Ratios are safe if both volumes use the same unit.
  • Not checking which quantity is constant before choosing the relationship.
  • Using 273.15 unnecessarily — National 5 uses 273.

Practice

1

A fixed mass of gas at 120 kPa and 20 °C is heated to 40 °C at constant volume. Find the new pressure.

Show answer

$T_1=20+273=293\ ext{K}$; $T_2=40+273=313\ ext{K}$.

$p_2=\dfrac{p_1T_2}{T_1}=\dfrac{120 imes313}{293}=128.2\ ext{kPa}$.

New pressure = $1.3 imes10^2$ kPa (2 s.f.).

2

A fixed mass of gas occupies 500 cm³ at 27 °C. It is cooled at constant pressure until its volume is 200 cm³. Find the new temperature in °C.

Show answer

$T_1=27+273=300\ ext{K}$.

$T_2=\dfrac{T_1V_2}{V_1}=\dfrac{300 imes200}{500}=120\ ext{K}$.

$120\ ext{K}=120-273=-153\ ^\circ ext{C}$.

New temperature = $-153$ °C.

17Wave parameters & behaviour

⚖️ Either paper

▶ YouTube

💡 The rule

  • Wave speed: $v=f\lambda$; also $v=\dfrac{d}{t}$.
  • Frequency: $f=\dfrac{N}{t}$; period: $T=\dfrac{1}{f}$.
  • Waves transfer energy without transferring matter.
  • Transverse waves vibrate at right angles to the direction of travel (e.g. water waves, EM radiation). Longitudinal waves vibrate along the direction of travel (e.g. sound).
  • Diffraction is the spreading of waves when they pass through a gap or around an object; it is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength.
  • Units: $v$ in m s$^{-1}$, $f$ in Hz, $\lambda$ in m, $T$ in s; $N$ (number of waves) has no unit.

📝 Worked example

A wave has a frequency of 50 Hz and a wavelength of 0.20 m. Find its speed and period.

$v=f\lambda=50\times0.20=10\ \text{m s}^{-1}$

$T=\dfrac{1}{f}=\dfrac{1}{50}=0.020\ \text{s}$

Wave speed = 10 m s$^{-1}$; period = 0.020 s (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Confusing frequency and period. $T=1/f$, not $f=1/T$ (unless rearranged correctly).
  • Using wavelength in cm instead of m.
  • Not converting kHz to Hz. 1 kHz $=1\times10^3$ Hz.
  • Counting the wrong number of waves when using $f=N/t$.
  • Confusing amplitude with wavelength. Amplitude is the maximum displacement from the rest position, not the distance between peaks.

Practice

1

A wave has a frequency of 2000 Hz and a wavelength of 0.15 m. Find its speed.

Show answer

$v=f\lambda=2000 imes0.15=300\ ext{m s}^{-1}$.

Wave speed = 300 m s$^{-1}$ (2 s.f.).

2

12 complete waves pass a point in 3.0 s. Find the frequency and the period.

Show answer

$f=\dfrac{N}{t}=\dfrac{12}{3.0}=4.0\ ext{Hz}$; $T=\dfrac{1}{f}=\dfrac{1}{4.0}=0.25\ ext{s}$.

Frequency = 4.0 Hz; period = 0.25 s.

18The electromagnetic spectrum

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Waves | Electromagnetic Spectrum | THEORY

💡 The rule

  • All electromagnetic (EM) waves are transverse and travel at the speed of light in a vacuum: $c=3.0\times10^8$ m s$^{-1}$.
  • Using $v=f\lambda$ with $v=c$ gives $c=f\lambda$, so $f=\dfrac{c}{\lambda}$.
  • Order of increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength): radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma.
  • Visible light has wavelengths from about $4\times10^{-7}$ m to $7\times10^{-7}$ m.
  • Units: $f$ in Hz, $\lambda$ in m.
  • Uses: radio (communication), microwave (satellite links, cooking), infrared (heating, remote controls), visible (sight, photography), ultraviolet (sterilising), X-ray (imaging), gamma (cancer treatment).

📝 Worked example

Green light has a wavelength of $5.4\times10^{-7}$ m. Find its frequency.

$f=\dfrac{c}{\lambda}=\dfrac{3.0\times10^8}{5.4\times10^{-7}}=5.556\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$

$f\approx5.6\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$

Frequency = $5.6\times10^{14}$ Hz (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using the speed of sound (340 m/s) instead of the speed of light.
  • Forgetting to convert nm to m. 1 nm $=1\times10^{-9}$ m.
  • Ordering the spectrum wrongly. Radio has the lowest frequency; gamma the highest.
  • Thinking different EM waves travel at different speeds in a vacuum. They all travel at $c$.
  • Confusing ionising and non-ionising radiation. Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are ionising.

Practice

1

A radio wave has a frequency of 100 MHz. Find its wavelength.

Show answer

$f=100\ ext{MHz}=1.0 imes10^8\ ext{Hz}$.

$\lambda=\dfrac{c}{f}=\dfrac{3.0 imes10^8}{1.0 imes10^8}=3.0\ ext{m}$.

Wavelength = 3.0 m (2 s.f.).

2

An X-ray has a wavelength of $1.0 imes10^{-10}$ m. Find its frequency.

Show answer

$f=\dfrac{c}{\lambda}=\dfrac{3.0 imes10^8}{1.0 imes10^{-10}}=3.0 imes10^{18}\ ext{Hz}$.

Frequency = $3.0 imes10^{18}$ Hz (2 s.f.).

19Refraction of light

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Waves | Refraction Experiments

💡 The rule

  • Refraction is the change in speed (and wavelength) of a wave when it passes from one medium into another. If the angle of incidence is greater than 0°, the wave also changes direction.
  • The frequency stays constant across the boundary, so because $v=f\lambda$, the wavelength changes when the speed changes.
  • Going into a more dense medium (e.g. air → glass): speed decreases, wavelength decreases, and the ray bends towards the normal.
  • Going into a less dense medium: speed increases, wavelength increases, and the ray bends away from the normal.
  • Speeds from the data sheet: air $3.0\times10^8$ m s$^{-1}$, glass $2.0\times10^8$ m s$^{-1}$, water $2.3\times10^8$ m s$^{-1}$.

📝 Worked example

Light of wavelength 600 nm passes from air into glass. Find its frequency and its wavelength in the glass.

$f=\dfrac{c}{\lambda}=\dfrac{3.0\times10^8}{600\times10^{-9}}=5.0\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$

In glass the speed is $2.0\times10^8$ m s$^{-1}$, so:

$\lambda_{\text{glass}}=\dfrac{v}{f}=\dfrac{2.0\times10^8}{5.0\times10^{14}}=4.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$

The ray bends towards the normal.

Frequency = $5.0\times10^{14}$ Hz (unchanged); wavelength in glass = $4.0\times10^{-7}$ m (400 nm).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Saying the frequency changes. Frequency is unchanged during refraction; speed and wavelength change.
  • Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal. Always measure incidence and refraction angles from the normal (a dashed line at 90° to the surface).
  • Saying the ray bends away from the normal when entering a denser medium. It bends towards the normal.
  • Using the wrong speed for the medium.
  • Forgetting that the wavelength changes at the boundary.

Practice

1

Light passes from water (2.3×10⁸ m/s) into air (3.0×10⁸ m/s). State and explain what happens to its speed, wavelength and direction.

Show answer

Speed increases (from $2.3 imes10^8$ to $3.0 imes10^8$ m s$^{-1}$). Frequency is constant, so by $v=f\lambda$ the wavelength increases. The ray bends away from the normal (provided the angle of incidence is greater than 0°).

Speed increases; wavelength increases; ray bends away from the normal.

2

Light of wavelength 500 nm passes from air into water (speed in water = 2.3×10⁸ m/s). Find its wavelength in the water.

Show answer

$f=\dfrac{c}{\lambda_{ ext{air}}}=\dfrac{3.0 imes10^8}{500 imes10^{-9}}=6.0 imes10^{14}\ ext{Hz}$.

$\lambda_{ ext{water}}=\dfrac{v_{ ext{water}}}{f}=\dfrac{2.3 imes10^8}{6.0 imes10^{14}}=3.83 imes10^{-7}\ ext{m}$.

Wavelength in water = $3.8 imes10^{-7}$ m (380 nm) (2 s.f.).

20Nuclear radiation

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Radiation | What You Should Know

💡 The rule

  • Alpha (α): 2 protons + 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus), charge +2. Highly ionising; stopped by paper or a few centimetres of air.
  • Beta (β): a fast electron, charge −1. Medium ionising; stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.
  • Gamma (γ): electromagnetic radiation, no charge. Weakly ionising; stopped by thick lead or concrete.
  • Ionisation is the removal of electrons from neutral atoms, creating ions. It can damage living cells.
  • Activity: $A=\dfrac{N}{t}$, where $N$ is the number of nuclear disintegrations and $t$ is time. Unit: becquerel (Bq) = 1 disintegration per second.
  • Sources of background radiation include radon gas, rocks, cosmic rays, food and medical sources.

📝 Worked example

A radioactive source produces 6000 disintegrations in 200 s. Find its activity.

$A=\dfrac{N}{t}=\dfrac{6000}{200}=30\ \text{Bq}$

Activity = 30 Bq (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Confusing the penetration order. Alpha is stopped first (paper); gamma is the most penetrating (lead).
  • Thinking gamma is charged. Gamma is electromagnetic radiation with no charge.
  • Forgetting that activity is disintegrations per second, not the total count.
  • Using minutes without converting to seconds.
  • Mixing up ionising ability and penetration. Alpha is the most ionising but the least penetrating.

Practice

1

A source produces 1.5×10⁴ disintegrations in 500 s. Find its activity.

Show answer

$A=\dfrac{N}{t}=\dfrac{1.5 imes10^4}{500}=30\ ext{Bq}$.

Activity = 30 Bq (2 s.f.).

2

A source's radiation passes through paper but is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. Identify the radiation and explain your answer.

Show answer

The radiation is beta. Alpha would be stopped by the paper, while gamma would pass through the aluminium. Beta passes through paper but is stopped by aluminium.

Beta radiation.

21Half-life

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Radiation | Half-life | WORKED EXAMPLES

💡 The rule

  • Half-life is the time taken for half of the nuclei in a radioactive sample (or half its activity/count rate) to decay.
  • After $n$ half-lives: $A=A_0\left(\dfrac12\right)^n$, where $n=\dfrac{\text{total time}}{\text{half-life}}$.
  • Half-life is constant regardless of sample size, temperature or chemical state.
  • Units: time in s, minutes, hours, days or years; activity in Bq.
  • To find half-life from a graph, read the time taken for the activity to halve.

📝 Worked example

A source has an initial activity of 800 Bq and a half-life of 6.0 hours. Find its activity after 18 hours.

$n=\dfrac{18}{6.0}=3$ half-lives

$A=\dfrac{A_0}{2^n}=\dfrac{800}{2^3}=\dfrac{800}{8}=100\ \text{Bq}$

Activity after 18 hours = 100 Bq (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Dividing by 2 once instead of by $2^n$. After 3 half-lives the activity is divided by 8, not 2.
  • Calculating $n$ incorrectly. $n=$ total time ÷ half-life.
  • Rounding too early between successive halvings.
  • Reading a graph at the wrong point. The half-life is the time for the activity to halve, not to reach zero.
  • Ignoring background count rate when using count-rate data.

Practice

1

An isotope has an initial activity of 4000 Bq and a half-life of 5 days. Find its activity after 20 days.

Show answer

$n=\dfrac{20}{5}=4$ half-lives.

$A=\dfrac{4000}{2^4}=\dfrac{4000}{16}=250\ ext{Bq}$.

Activity = 250 Bq (2 s.f.).

2

A 64 g sample of a radioactive material decays until only 8 g remains. How many half-lives have passed?

Show answer

$64 o32 o16 o8$ is 3 halvings.

3 half-lives have passed.

22Dose, equivalent dose & safety

⚖️ Either paper

▶ National 5 Physics | Radiation | Equivalent Dose | THEORY

💡 The rule

  • Absorbed dose: $D=\dfrac{E}{m}$ — energy absorbed per unit mass. Unit: gray (Gy), 1 Gy = 1 J kg$^{-1}$.
  • Equivalent dose: $H=D w_r$ — absorbed dose weighted for the biological harm of the radiation type. Unit: sievert (Sv), 1 Sv = 1 J kg$^{-1}$.
  • Equivalent dose rate: $\dot{H}=\dfrac{H}{t}$, in Sv s$^{-1}$ or µSv h$^{-1}$.
  • Radiation weighting factors (data sheet): alpha 20, beta 1, fast neutrons 10, gamma 1, slow neutrons 3.
  • UK average background radiation ≈ 2.2 mSv per year; annual effective dose limit for the public 1 mSv; for radiation workers 20 mSv.

📝 Worked example

0.50 kg of tissue absorbs 0.030 J of alpha radiation. Find the absorbed dose and the equivalent dose.

$D=\dfrac{E}{m}=\dfrac{0.030}{0.50}=0.060\ \text{Gy}$

$H=D w_r=0.060\times20=1.2\ \text{Sv}$

Absorbed dose = 0.060 Gy; equivalent dose = 1.2 Sv (2 s.f.).

🚨 Exam trap

  • Using mass in grams instead of kilograms.
  • Forgetting to multiply by the radiation weighting factor. For alpha and neutrons, Gy ≠ Sv.
  • Mixing Gy and Sv. Absorbed dose is measured in grays; equivalent dose in sieverts.
  • Not converting mSv to Sv. 1 mSv $=1\times10^{-3}$ Sv.
  • Treating 2.2 mSv as a legal limit. It is the average annual background radiation, not a limit.
  • Not converting time units when calculating an equivalent dose rate.

Practice

1

2.0 kg of tissue absorbs 0.0040 J of gamma radiation. Find the absorbed dose and the equivalent dose.

Show answer

$D=\dfrac{0.0040}{2.0}=2.0 imes10^{-3}\ ext{Gy}$.

For gamma, $w_r=1$, so $H=D w_r=2.0 imes10^{-3}\ ext{Sv}=2.0\ ext{mSv}$.

Absorbed dose = $2.0 imes10^{-3}$ Gy; equivalent dose = 2.0 mSv.

2

A worker receives an absorbed dose of 0.0010 Gy from alpha radiation. Find the equivalent dose.

Show answer

$H=D w_r=0.0010 imes20=0.020\ ext{Sv}$.

Equivalent dose = 0.020 Sv = 20 mSv.
